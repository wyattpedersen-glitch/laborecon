Highest-value habits
  • Always take logs before differentiating for elasticities. \(\partial \log L/\partial \log w\) is the elasticity. This saves you from product-rule messes.
  • State the sign condition. Nearly every comparative static in this course has a parameter condition (\(\rho>0\), \(\gamma<0\), \(v''<0\), \(\nu>\rho\), \(w<rA\)). The condition is the answer.
  • For papers, lead with the identification strategy. Name the design, name the assumption, say whether you believe it and why, then the number, then which model.

Lecture 2 · Competitive equilibrium

The simplest model in the course and the foundation for everything after: workers with reservation wages, a price-taking firm, and the wage that clears the market.

1. Labor supply (extensive margin)

A continuum of workers \(i\in[0,N]\). Each chooses employment \(e\in\{0,1\}\) and consumption \(C\):

\[ u_i(C,e)=v(C)-e\,c_i,\qquad C\le \pi + e\,w, \]

where \(\pi\) is non-labor income and \(c_i\) is \(i\)'s disutility of work. Worker \(i\) works iff \(v(\pi+w)-c_i\ge v(\pi)\). The reservation wage \(w^r_i\) solves

\[ v(\pi+w^r_i)-c_i=v(\pi). \]

The labor supply schedule is the number of workers whose reservation wage is below the going wage:

\[ L^s(w)=N\,P[w^r_i\le w]. \]
Three things to be able to say about this supply curve.
  • It can never slope down: a CDF is weakly increasing. By modelling only whether people work (extensive margin) we have ruled out a backward-bending supply curve.
  • Assumptions: extensive margin only; homogeneous jobs; homogeneous workers. Whether they matter depends on the question.
  • The unearned-income effect is ambiguous. Raise \(\pi\): both sides of \(v(\pi+w^r)-c_i=v(\pi)\) rise. If \(v''<0\) (diminishing marginal utility), the left side rises by less, so \(w^r_i\) must increase and labor supply falls. If \(v''>0\), the reverse. The model makes no prediction without a stance on \(v''\).

2. Labor demand

A representative price-taking firm with production \(y(L)\), output price \(p\) exogenous:

\[ L^D(w)=\arg\max_L\{p\,y(L)-wL\}\quad\Rightarrow\quad w=p\,y'(L^D(w)). \]

Wages equal the marginal revenue product of labor. The inverse demand curve is \(w^D(L)=p\,y'(L)\). We assume \(y''\le 0\), so demand does not slope up (otherwise the second-order condition fails and you would need imperfect competition to make sense of it).

  • An increase in \(p\) shifts inverse demand up: \(w=p\cdot y'(L)\) with \(w\) fixed and \(p\) up requires \(y'(L)\) down, hence \(L\) up.
  • Multiple firms: add demand curves horizontally (sum the \(L\)'s at each \(w\)).
  • Ignoring capital is fine: reinterpret \(y(L)\) as a value-added production function with other inputs chosen optimally (HW1 Q1c).
  • Partial equilibrium: \(p\) is exogenous. For non-tradeables, \(p\) might fall as output rises.

3. Equilibrium and shocks

\[ L=N\,P[w^r_i\le w]\quad(\text{supply}),\qquad w=p\,y'(L)\quad(\text{demand}). \]

Demand shock (Trans-Alaska pipeline, 1974–77)

Construction employment rose from ≈8,000 to ≈30,000 (+275%); monthly earnings from ≈$4,000 to ≈$7,000 (+75%). Implied supply elasticity ≈ 275/75 ≈ 3.7. Large because the margin is inter-state migration, not employment vs unemployment. We used a demand shock to trace out supply.

Supply shock (Czech commuters, Dustmann–Schönberg–Stuhler)

\(N\uparrow\) shifts supply right: \(w\downarrow\), \(L\uparrow\). Empirically a 1% supply shock cut native wages ≈0.1% and native employment ≈0.9% (mostly fewer natives flowing in). Note: commuters did not live in Germany, so there was no offsetting demand increase.

Immigration ≠ pure supply shock

Immigrants also consume locally, shifting labor demand out. Quasi-experimental studies (Card 1990; Dustmann et al. 2016 JEP) generally find small wage effects. Draw both curves shifting right: wages can be unchanged while employment rises.

4. Why "just run OLS of employment on wages" fails

\(\log \text{emp}_c=\alpha+\beta\log w_c+u_c\) has two problems: omitted variable bias (rural areas are less productive but nicer places to be unemployed) and reverse causality / simultaneity (a market with high \(u_c\) has more employment, which lowers wages via decreasing returns, so you may be tracing demand, not supply). Hence RCTs and quasi-experiments.

6. The HW1 Q2 worked model (know this cold)

\(v(C)=\log C\), unearned income \(b\), disutility \(v_i\) with \(P[v_i<x]=a\exp(\lambda x)\), firm \(y=L^\gamma\).

  • Work iff \(\log w - v_i\ge\log b\); reservation wage \(w^r_i=b\,e^{v_i}\).
  • \[L^s=N\,P[b e^{v_i}<w]=Na\exp(\lambda(\log w-\log b))\] so \(\log L^s=\log(aN)+\lambda(\log w-\log b)\). Supply elasticity \(=\lambda\).

  • Equate and solve: \[\log w=\frac{\gamma-1}{\lambda(\gamma-1)-1}\big(\lambda\log b-\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}\log p-\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}\log\gamma-\log a-\log N\big)\].

  • Do not say "higher unearned income lowers labor supply" as if the model implies it. It depends on \(v''\). With \(v=\log\) (HW1), \(v''<0\), so yes; in general, no.
  • The Paycheck Plus and Alaska elasticities measure different margins (unemployment→employment vs migration). Different elasticities are not a contradiction.
  • "Elasticity" means \(\partial\log L/\partial\log w\). Compute it from a log-linear form, never from levels.

Lecture 3 · Technological change

Two models with opposite predictions for wages, one decisive table, and the evidence that says which one fits which technology.

1. The facts slides (know the "general thrust")

  • Productivity–pay divergence. Since ≈1973 US labor productivity (GDP ÷ hours) has roughly tripled (≈1,700 → ≈4,700 in 2026 $/week) while the median full-time wage barely moved (≈1,100 → ≈1,300). Before 1973 they rose together.
  • Falling labor share. Compensation of employees as a share of GDP fell from ≈0.64 (1970) to ≈0.57 (2023), with a bump around 2000.
  • Rising inequality. Real weekly earnings relative to 1961: 90th percentile ≈ +70%, 10th ≈ +40%, median ≈ +20%. By education: grad-school earnings ≈ +40%, bachelor's ≈ +15%, high school ≈ −10%, less than high school ≈ −15 to −20%, with the fan-out beginning around 1980.
  • Job polarization (Autor 2019): since 1970, employment shares of production, operators/laborers, and office/admin fell (production −25pp cumulative); professionals, technicians, managers and health/personal services rose.

2. Common setup

Labor supply \(L^s(w)\) (not microfounded), a price-taking firm, output price normalized to 1, and

\[ y(L)=(AL)^\gamma,\qquad \gamma\in(0,1), \]

where \(AL\) is "effective labor" and \(A\) is labor productivity. Inverse labor demand is the marginal product:

\[ w^D(L)=\frac{\partial}{\partial L}(AL)^\gamma=\gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}. \]

3. Model 1 · Labor-augmenting technological change (\(A\uparrow\))

Raising \(A\) shifts the whole inverse demand curve \(\gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\) up. Equilibrium wages and employment both rise. Average productivity:

\[ \frac{y}{L}=\frac{(AL)^\gamma}{L}=A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}=\frac{w}{\gamma}, \]

so average productivity is proportional to the wage and rises with it.

4. Model 2 · Automation (labor-substituting change)

A machine \(M\) that is a perfect substitute for effective labor, each costing \(r\) with perfectly elastic supply:

\[ y(L,M)=(AL+M)^\gamma,\qquad \max_{L,M}\{(AL+M)^\gamma-wL-rM\}. \]

Split into cost minimization then output choice.

  1. Cost minimization. Holding output fixed, one machine replaces \(1/A\) units of labor, changing cost by \(r-w/A\). So: if \(w>rA\) the firm hires no workers; if \(w<rA\) it uses no machines; at \(w=rA\) it is indifferent.
  2. Output choice. With machines only, \(Y=M^\gamma\), \(c(Y)=rY^{1/\gamma}\), FOC \(1=\frac{r}{\gamma}Y^{1/\gamma-1}\Rightarrow Y=(\gamma/r)^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\). With labor only, \(c(Y)=\frac{w}{A}Y^{1/\gamma}\), \(Y=(A\gamma/w)^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)}\), and \(L=Y^{1/\gamma}/A\) rearranges to \(w=\gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\) again.
Result. Inverse labor demand after automation is \[ w^D(L)=\min\{\,rA,\ \gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\,\}. \] From the workers' side, automation looks like a maximum wage at \(rA\), binding whenever any machines are used. Wages and employment both fall.

Output rises. Before: \[c'(Y)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\frac{w_{\text{initial}}}{A}Y^{1/\gamma-1}\]. After (with \(rA<w_{\text{initial}}\) so machines are used): \(c'(Y)=\frac{1}{\gamma}rY^{1/\gamma-1}\), strictly lower at every \(Y\). Price = marginal cost, so \(Y\) must rise. Output up and employment down means average productivity rises here too.

6. Why the empirics are hard, and the two main papers

  • Adopters may be more efficient in other ways (selection). Ideally study equilibrium effects, but firm-level responses holding others fixed still teach a lot.
  • Caunedo & Kala (2022): RCT of equipment-rental vouchers in Karnataka, India. Firm-level: labor use per acre falls (family male −16%, hired female −12%), wages ≈ unchanged, output ≈ unchanged. No wage effect because treated farms are small relative to the local labor market, so aggregate demand did not move. Partial equilibrium by design.
  • Acemoglu & Restrepo (2020 JPE): equilibrium effects across US commuting zones 1990–2007. Exposure = Σ (1970 industry employment share) × (European adjusted robot penetration in that industry). IV: European penetration instruments US penetration. One more robot per 1,000 workers → employment/population −0.39pp and wages −0.77% (IV estimates). Consistent with automation.

Adjusted penetration for industry \(i\): \[\text{APR}_i=\frac{M_{i,t_1}-M_{i,t_0}}{L_{i,1990}}-g_i\frac{M_{i,t_0}}{L_{i,1990}}\]: robots per initial worker, net of robot growth that merely tracks output growth \(g_i\).

7. Extensions

  • New jobs. Technology that creates a new industry is exactly the L2 demand shock: demand curves add horizontally, wages and employment rise.
  • Skill bias. With two labor types (HW2 Q1), \(A_e\uparrow\) raises the educated wage premium iff \(\rho>0\) (sufficiently substitutable). See the L4 tab.
  • Long-run general equilibrium. With constant returns and every non-labor input perfectly elastically supplied, \(c'(y)\) is constant and equilibrium needs \(c'(y)=1\). A technology that lowers marginal cost must raise the price of some input, and the only input that can get more expensive is labor. So wages must rise "in the end".
Traps
  • Both models raise average productivity and output. They differ on marginal productivity, wages, and employment. The productivity–pay divergence fact is consistent with automation, not with labor-augmenting change.
  • Automation binds only if \(rA<w_{\text{initial}}\). If machines are too expensive the kink is above the old equilibrium and nothing happens.
  • Caunedo & Kala's zero wage effect does not contradict automation. It is what partial equilibrium predicts.

Lecture 4 · Education

Educated workers earn more. Three stories: human capital (school makes you productive), signaling (school proves you were already productive), selection (something else causes both). The evidence favors human capital, at least for high school.

1. The facts slides

  • Median real weekly earnings (2026 $) in 2024: grad school ≈ $2,100, bachelor's ≈ $1,650, associate's ≈ $1,200, high school ≈ $1,000, less than high school ≈ $800. The ordering has held since 1960; the gaps widened sharply after 1980, especially at the top (grad-school mean earnings jumped in the 1990s, more for men).
  • Less-than-high-school earnings fell in real terms after 1990.
  • Attainment (age 25+): bachelor's or higher rose from 4.6% (1940) to 32% (2014); less than high school fell from 75.9% to 11.7%.
  • Puzzle these facts set up: the relative supply of educated workers rose a lot and their relative wage rose too. In HW2 Q1 that combination implies educated and manual labor are substitutes (\(\rho>0\)) and skill-biased technical change drove demand.

2. Human capital theory

Trade-off: schooling raises future earnings but costs tuition \(H\) and foregone earnings. With no credit constraints, utility depends on education only through lifetime consumption, so the student picks the option with the higher net present value. Go to college iff

\[ \sum_{t=22}^{64}\frac{y_{COL}-y_{HS}}{(1+r)^{t-18}} \;>\; \sum_{t=18}^{21}\frac{y_{HS}}{(1+r)^{t-18}}+\sum_{t=18}^{21}\frac{H}{(1+r)^{t-18}}. \]

Depends on the premium \(y_{COL}-y_{HS}\), the interest rate \(r\), and tuition \(H\).

Two-period example from lecture

Uneducated earns $20,000 each period; educated earns $60,000 in period 2 only; tuition $4,000; \(r=100\%\). \(NPV_0=20{,}000+20{,}000/2=30{,}000\). \(NPV_1=-4{,}000+60{,}000/2=26{,}000\). Don't go.

Continuous schooling. Live forever, no tuition, earn \(y(S)\) after \(S\) years. Using \(\sum_{t\ge0}(1+r)^{-t}=\frac{1+r}{r}\):

\[ NPV(S)=\frac{y(S)}{(1+r)^S}\cdot\frac{1+r}{r}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \max_S\ \log y(S)-S\log(1+r)\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{y'(S)}{y(S)}=\log(1+r)\approx r. \]
Marginal rate of return = interest rate. The left side is the proportional earnings gain from one more year (the MRR). At the optimum it equals the return on working today and saving. Picture: a downward-sloping MRR curve crossed by a flat line at \(r\).

3. Observed vs causal returns, and ability bias

The causal return is the earnings change if we induced someone to get more schooling. The observed return is the earnings gap across people with different schooling. People differ in schooling because of (a) different \(r\) or (b) different \(y(S)\). If only \(r\) differs, observed = causal. If \(y(S)\) differs, the observed return is contaminated: ability bias.

Model A: \(y_i(S)=\alpha_i+\beta S\)

FOC \(r=\frac{\beta}{\alpha_i+\beta S}\Rightarrow S_i^*=\frac1r-\frac{\alpha_i}{\beta}\). Higher ability gets less schooling (the LeBron / Bill Gates case: the dollar gain is the same for everyone, but high-\(\alpha\) people forego more). Observed return biased down.

Model B: \(y_i(S)=\alpha+\beta_i S\)

\(S_i^*=\frac1r-\frac{\alpha}{\beta_i}\). Higher \(\beta_i\) gets more schooling: they convert school into earnings better. Observed return biased up (the conventional story).

People likely vary in both, so the direction of ability bias is theoretically unclear. That is why we need a design.

4. Angrist & Krueger (1991): IV with quarter of birth

School-entry laws: start school in the fall of the calendar year you turn 6. Compulsory schooling: stay until your 16th birthday. So a child born January 2, 1930 could drop out with ≈9.5 years; one born December 31, 1930 needed ≈10.5. Quarter of birth shifts schooling for would-be dropouts.

  • Relevance: Q1-born have ≈0.1 fewer years of schooling (visible saw-tooth in the data). ✓
  • As good as random: quarter of birth independent of ability and family background? Bound, Jaeger & Baker (1995) point to small correlations with family income and health, plus weak-instrument problems. Questionable.
  • Exclusion: quarter of birth affects earnings only through schooling. Possible violations via age-at-school-entry effects.
  • Monotonicity: being born later never reduces schooling. Plausible.

Finding: Wald estimate of the return to a year of schooling = 0.102 vs OLS 0.071 (men born 1930–39: Δ log wage −0.0111 ÷ Δ schooling −0.1088). IV is slightly above OLS, so upward ability bias is not large. The estimate is a LATE for compliers (people on the dropout margin). Supports human capital over pure selection; cannot by itself rule out signaling.

5. Signaling

Two types, each half the population: \(H\) with productivity \(y_H\), \(L\) with \(y_L<y_H\). Employers cannot see type and pay expected productivity; with no information everyone gets \(\frac12(y_H+y_L)\). School costs \(c_H\) for high types and \(c_L>c_H\) for low types, teaches nothing, and is observable. Employer beliefs drive everything.

Separating equilibrium (only \(H\) goes)

Schooled paid \(y_H\), unschooled \(y_L\). \(H\) goes iff \(y_H-y_L>c_H\); \(L\) stays iff \(y_H-y_L<c_L\). Exists iff \[ c_H<y_H-y_L<c_L. \] Beliefs are confirmed, so it is sustainable.

Pooling equilibrium (nobody goes)

Need off-path beliefs: assume a schooled deviator is believed to be \(H\) and paid \(y_H\). Nobody deviates iff \(y_H-c_H<\frac{y_H+y_L}{2}\), i.e. \[ y_H-y_L<2c_H. \] (The \(L\) condition is implied since \(c_L>c_H\).)

  • Multiple equilibria if \(c_H<y_H-y_L<\max\{c_L,2c_H\}\): each high type only wants school if the other high types go.
  • Schooling is pure social waste here: it redistributes from \(L\) to \(H\) and burns \(c_H\). Banning school is a Pareto improvement iff \(c_H<y_H-y_L<\min\{c_L,2c_H\}\): low types prefer pooling, and high types would rather be paid the average than pay \(c_H\) but must attend to avoid the "low" label.
  • Policy contrast. Human capital: schooling is productive; subsidize if credit constraints or externalities. Signaling: schooling is wasteful; possibly discourage it. Hence the stakes of testing which is right.

6. Clark & Martorell (2014): RD on the last-chance exam

Randomly assigning schooling cannot separate learning from credentials. You need to randomize the credential holding learning fixed. Texas students who repeatedly failed the exit exam take a last-chance exam; those barely passing get a diploma, those barely failing do not, with essentially identical learning.

  • Design: (fuzzy) regression discontinuity. Diploma receipt jumps from ≈0.4 to ≈0.9 at the cutoff, so passing is used as an IV for the diploma while controlling for flexible functions of the score.
  • Assumption: \(E[\nu_i\mid X_i]\) continuous at the cutoff: students cannot precisely manipulate scores. The density of scores is smooth at zero (no bunching), so this looks fine.
  • Finding: a diploma has no detectable effect on earnings: ≈ $100–180/year (1–2%), insignificant, PDV through year 11 ≈ +$600–1,700 (0.8–2%), not significant, with CIs that rule out a large signaling value.
  • Interpretation: signaling does not explain why high-school graduates earn more. Graduates do earn more than non-graduates with 12 years of school, so a diploma correlates with ability, but firms seem to infer productivity from other sources.
Putting it together. Angrist & Krueger: large causal returns to years of schooling, so selection is not the whole story. Clark & Martorell: the credential itself is worth ≈0, so signaling is not the story either. What remains is human capital, at least for high school.

7. HW2 Q1: skill-biased technical change (CES)

\(Y=\big((A_eL_e)^\rho+(A_mL_m)^\rho\big)^{\nu/\rho}\). Wages equal marginal products, and the ratio is

\[ \frac{w_e}{w_m}=\Big(\frac{A_e}{A_m}\Big)^{\rho}\Big(\frac{L_e}{L_m}\Big)^{\rho-1}. \]
  • \(A_e\uparrow\) raises the premium iff \(\rho>0\) (sufficiently substitutable). If poor substitutes (\(\rho<0\)), more productive educated workers make manual workers relatively scarce and raise \(w_m\) more.
  • Supply: worker goes to school iff \(\log w_e-v_i>\log w_m\) with logistic \(v_i\) (scale \(a\)): \(L_e/L_m=(w_e/w_m)^a\).
  • Equilibrium: \[\frac{w_e}{w_m}=\big(\frac{A_e}{A_m}\big)^{\frac{\rho}{1-a(\rho-1)}}\], \[\frac{L_e}{L_m}=\big(\frac{A_e}{A_m}\big)^{\frac{a\rho}{1-a(\rho-1)}}\]. Denominator is positive (\(a>0,\rho<1\)), so both rise with \(A_e\) iff \(\rho>0\). Recent decades saw both rise ⇒ \(\rho>0\).
  • Does SBTC raise manual wages? \(\partial\log w_m/\partial A_e\propto\nu-\rho\). With decreasing returns (\(\nu<1\)) and near-perfect substitutes (\(\rho\approx1\)) it can lower \(w_m\).
Traps
  • In Model A higher ability means less schooling. Students often assume ability bias is always upward. Say "direction is theoretically ambiguous" and give both models.
  • Pooling requires specifying off-path beliefs. If you forget them, you cannot check whether deviating pays.
  • Clark & Martorell test signaling value of the diploma, not returns to years of school. A zero there and a positive A&K estimate are complementary, not contradictory.
  • IV identifies a LATE: the return for people whose schooling was moved by the instrument (near-dropouts), not the average student.

Lecture 5 · Compensating differentials

Adam Smith's claim: unpleasant jobs pay more. The raw data say the opposite. The model explains why, and three papers show the differential appears once you hold the worker fixed.

1. The facts slides

  • Work got much safer. Deaths per 100,000 workers: ≈61 in 1913, ≈37 in 1933, ≈9 by 1990, ≈3.5 today.
  • Flexibility rose. Share able to vary start/end times: ≈12% (1985) → ≈28% (late 1990s–2004) → ≈55% (2017–18, ATUS).
  • Work from home: ≈7% in 1960, ≈2–3% in 1980, ≈5% in 2019, jumped to ≈18% in 2021 and settled ≈13% by 2024.
  • Paid leave: paid vacation access flat ≈75–82% since 1992; paid sick leave rose from ≈50% (1992) to ≈80% (2025).
  • Raw cross-section (RAND AWCS 2015): better jobs pay more. Tiring/painful positions ≥ half the time: $34/hr vs $52 without. Can choose work location: $46 vs $40. Can choose task order: $52 vs $33. Paid vacation: $52 vs $37. The theory "isn't looking so good" until you control for worker productivity (amenities are a normal good) and other job characteristics.

2. The model

Workers \(i\in[0,1]\) choose the healthy industry \(H\) or the gross industry \(G\); no unemployment option.

  • Demand. \(H\) has constant returns \(y_H(L)=A_HL\), so \(w_H^D=A_H\): the healthy wage is pinned down (exogenous). \(G\) has decreasing returns \(y_G(L)\), so \(w_G^D(L_G)=y_G'(L_G)\).
  • Supply. \(u_i(C,r)=v(C)-a_i r\) with \(C=w_j\), risk \(r=0\) in \(H\) and \(r=r_G>0\) in \(G\), and \(a_i\ge0\) the disutility of risk. Choose \(G\) iff \(v(w_G)-r_Ga_i\ge v(A_H)\), i.e. \(a_i\le\frac{v(w_G)-v(A_H)}{r_G}\). So \[ L_G^s(w_G)=P\Big[a_i\le\frac{v(w_G)-v(A_H)}{r_G}\Big]. \]
The compensating differential is \(w_G^*-w_H^*\). It is the wage gap needed to compensate the marginal worker, so it is an equilibrium object, not a preference parameter.

3. Comparative statics (both increase the differential)

\(A_G\uparrow\) with \(y_G=A_GL_G^\gamma\)

\(y_G'=A_G\gamma L_G^{\gamma-1}\) shifts demand up. Move up the supply curve: \(L_G\uparrow\), \(w_G\uparrow\). The new marginal workers dislike risk more, so they need more compensation. Differential ↑.

\(r_G\uparrow\)

For any \(w_G>A_H\), \(\frac{v(w_G)-v(A_H)}{r_G}\) falls, so supply shifts left. Move up the demand curve: \(L_G\downarrow\), \(w_G\uparrow\). Differential ↑.

4. Value of a statistical life

\[ VSL=\frac{\text{change in earnings}}{\text{change in probability of death}}. \]

Used in cost–benefit analysis (pollution rules, health spending, road safety). The compensating differential is the wage change that compensates a marginal worker for a risk change, which is exactly the ratio above. Lee & Taylor's estimates: roughly $6–9 million per statistical life.

6. HW2 Q2: heterogeneous productivity and why \(\gamma<0\)

Workers have productivity \(\theta_i\) and \(v(C)=C^\gamma/\gamma\). Utility in \(G\): \((\theta A_G)^\gamma/\gamma-a_i\); in \(H\): \((\theta A_H)^\gamma/\gamma\). With \(a_i\sim U[0,1]\):

\[ P[\text{works in }G\mid\theta]=\frac{\theta^\gamma}{\gamma}\big(A_G^\gamma-A_H^\gamma\big). \]
  • Increasing in \(\theta\) iff \(\gamma>0\) (because \(A_G>A_H\) makes \(A_G^\gamma-A_H^\gamma\) and \(\gamma\) share a sign).
  • Ratio for high vs low types \(=(\theta_{high}/\theta_{low})^\gamma\), which is \(<1\) iff \(\gamma<0\): low-productivity workers sort into the gross industry.
  • Then \[\frac{E[\text{earnings in }H]}{E[\text{earnings in }G]}=\frac{A_H}{A_G}\cdot\frac{E[\theta\mid H]}{E[\theta\mid G]}\ne\frac{A_H}{A_G}\]: the raw wage ratio mixes the causal differential with productivity sorting. This is exactly the bias the AWCS bar charts suffer from.
  • Facts (amenities improving over time as incomes rise; higher-paid workers have better amenities) are both consistent with \(\gamma<0\): amenities are a normal good.
  • A change in the share of each type has no effect on these probabilities because each industry's \(A\) is fixed, i.e. we implicitly assumed constant returns in both industries.

7. Endogenous amenities

Firms choose vacation policies, safety, WFH. This does not change the core result: the differential still equals the marginal worker's WTP. But if less productive workers have stronger preferences for an amenity and productivity is hard to observe, offering it attracts an adversely selected pool, so firms may not offer an efficient bundle (Palmer 2025; Emanuel & Harrington 2024).

Traps
  • "Better jobs pay more in the data, so compensating differentials are wrong." No: productivity sorting (\(\gamma<0\)) and correlated job characteristics bias the raw comparison. State the bias, then cite Lavetti / Lee–Taylor.
  • The differential compensates the marginal worker, not the average worker. This is why Mas & Pallais can find high WTP for some people and still small market differentials.
  • In the Lee–Taylor IV, the exclusion restriction is that inspections move wages only via risk. They check that inspections don't move productivity or other outcomes much; mention this as the assumption to worry about.

Four research designs

For each: when it applies, what it estimates, the assumption that makes the number causal, and how to check it. The exam will ask you to apply these to a paper.

The underlying problem

We want the effect of a treatment on an outcome. For each person we see only the treated or the untreated outcome, never both. Naive comparisons (OLS of outcome on treatment) fail because of omitted variables (confounders drive both) and reverse causality (outcome drives treatment). Each design finds variation in treatment that is as good as random.

1. Randomized controlled trial

Paycheck Plus · OpenResearch · Caunedo–Kala · Mas–Pallais · Lee–Taylor (random inspections)
Idea
Randomly assign treatment. Then treated and control groups are identical in expectation, so \[\text{ATE}\approx\bar Y_{\text{treated}}-\bar Y_{\text{control}}\].
Estimates
The average treatment effect in the study population.
Assumptions
Randomization actually worked (check covariate balance). No spillovers from treated to control units (SUTVA). If some assigned to treatment don't take it up, the raw comparison is an intention-to-treat effect.
Limits
External validity: the effect for low-income single New Yorkers or Karnataka smallholders may not generalize. Partial equilibrium: if treated units are small relative to the market, you will not see wage effects (Caunedo & Kala). Ethics and cost.

2. Instrumental variables

Acemoglu–Restrepo · Angrist–Krueger · fuzzy RD (Clark–Martorell) · Lee–Taylor second stage
Idea
Effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) is confounded by \(C\). Find an instrument \(Z\) that moves \(X\) and affects \(Y\) only through \(X\). Then \[ \beta_{IV}=\frac{\delta}{\pi}=\frac{\text{effect of }Z\text{ on }Y\ (\text{reduced form})}{\text{effect of }Z\text{ on }X\ (\text{first stage})}. \]
Four assumptions
  1. Relevance: \(Z\) correlates with \(X\) (testable: first-stage F-stat; A&R ≈ 35).
  2. As good as random: no confounder links \(Z\) and \(Y\).
  3. Exclusion: \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only via \(X\).
  4. Monotonicity: \(Z\) never pushes \(X\) in opposite directions for different people.
Estimates
A local average treatment effect (LATE): the average effect among compliers, those whose \(X\) was moved by \(Z\). Angrist–Krueger's return is for near-dropouts, not for everyone.
Worked example
A&R: \(X\) = US robot exposure, \(Y\) = US employment/wages, \(C\) = e.g. population aging or local demand shocks, \(Z\) = European robot penetration mapped to US commuting zones through 1970 industry shares. Relevance: strong. As-good-as-random/exclusion: requires that whatever drives European adoption in an industry is not separately affecting US local labor markets (controls for Chinese import exposure, other industry trends; pre-trends flat 1970–90). Monotonicity: more European robots never reduce US robots. Reasonable.
Worked example
A&K: \(X\) = years of schooling, \(Y\) = log earnings, \(C\) = ability, \(Z\) = quarter of birth. Relevance weak-ish (0.1 year). Exclusion debatable (age at school entry). Result 0.102 vs OLS 0.071.

3. Regression discontinuity

Clark–Martorell (test cutoff) · Gaggl–Wright (50-employee cutoff)
Idea
Treatment switches on at a cutoff \(c\) of a running variable: \(D_i=1\{X_i\ge c\}\). People just above and just below are alike except for treatment.
Setup
\(Y_i=\alpha+\beta D_i+\nu_i\). Problem: \(D_i\) may correlate with \(\nu_i\). Key assumption: \(E[\nu_i\mid X_i]\) is continuous at \(X_i=c\).
Derivation
\(\lim_{x\to c^-}E[Y\mid X=x]=\alpha+E[\nu\mid X=c]\) because \(D=0\) left of the cutoff; \(\lim_{x\to c^+}E[Y\mid X=x]=\alpha+\beta+E[\nu\mid X=c]\). Difference of limits \(=\beta\).
Checks
No manipulation of the running variable: the density of \(X\) should be smooth at \(c\) (no bunching). Covariates should not jump at \(c\). Clark & Martorell pass both; Gaggl & Wright find no bunching at 50 employees and the policy was unanticipated.
Fuzzy RD
When crossing \(c\) raises the probability of treatment but not to 1 (diploma receipt 0.4 → 0.9), use \(1\{X\ge c\}\) as an instrument for \(D\) while controlling flexibly for \(X\). It is IV with the cutoff as the instrument.
Estimates
The effect at the cutoff: a local effect for marginal passers / firms near 50 employees.

4. Difference-in-differences

Dustmann–Schönberg–Stuhler · Lee–Taylor event study · (Alaska pipeline, informally)
Idea
Treated and control units observed before and after treatment. The treated unit differs from the control, and its outcome might have changed anyway, but the difference in the changes is attributed to treatment: \[ \hat\beta=(Y^{1}_{T,1}-Y^{0}_{T,0})-(Y^{0}_{C,1}-Y^{0}_{C,0}). \]
Key assumption
Parallel trends: absent treatment, treated and control would have moved in parallel. Not directly testable, but pre-trends (placebo estimates for periods before treatment) should be flat.
Regression form
Two-way fixed effects: \(Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\delta_t+\beta T_{it}+u_{it}\). \(\alpha_i\) absorbs anything constant within unit; \(\delta_t\) anything common to all units in a period. With 2 units × 2 periods this is exactly the DiD; with more it is close, and newer estimators (de Chaisemartin & D'Haultfœuille 2020) average many 2×2 comparisons.
Event study
Estimate \(\beta_h\) for each horizon \(h\) relative to treatment; normalize \(\beta_{-1}=0\); the pre-period \(\beta_h\) are placebo tests of parallel trends. Lee & Taylor's wage plot: flat before inspection, drops ≈ $0.2–0.35 after.
Worked example
DSS: border vs inland German districts, before/after 1991 commuting opening. Pre-trends flat. Threat: reunification shocks hitting border regions differently. Finding: 1% supply shock → wages −0.13%, native employment −0.93%.

Lecture 2 · Equilibrium

(a) Paycheck Plus — boosting the EITC for singles

RCT
Setting
New York City, 2013–2016. ≈6,000 low-income single adults without dependent children (who get almost nothing from the federal EITC: max ≈$510). Offered an earnings bonus of up to $2,000/year with a 30% phase-in, flat region, and 17% phase-out ending at ≈$29,900.
Design
Randomized controlled trial: 2,997 program, 2,971 control. Compare average outcomes. Assumptions: randomization balanced the groups; no spillovers. Partial take-up means this is an intention-to-treat effect. Plausible: it is a clean experiment.
Findings
After-bonus earnings +$635/year (3-year average); employment ("any earnings") +1.9pp (75.4% → 77.3%); pre-bonus earnings essentially unchanged. The bonus raised the effective wage ≈20% (2,000/10,186) and employment ≈2.5% (1.9/75.4), so the extensive-margin labor supply elasticity ≈ 0.125. Small, like most estimates (Chetty et al. 2012).
Model
Consistent with the L2 reservation-wage model: supply slopes up but is inelastic on the employment margin. Contrast the Alaska pipeline elasticity (≈3.7) which operates through migration.

(b) OpenResearch Unconditional Income Study

RCT
Setting
Texas and Illinois, 2020–2023. Low-income adults (ages 21–40). Treatment: $1,000/month for three years regardless of work; control: $50/month (a small "treatment" to encourage survey response). Average control household income ≈$48k, so the transfer would raise income ≈25% absent behavioral change.
Design
RCT. Outcomes from surveys and administrative UI records. Assumptions: random assignment, no spillovers. Plausible. Note the contrast with EITC-type studies: this changes unearned income, not the return to work.
Findings
Employment −2pp (survey, control mean 74%), −7pp (UI data, control 61%), pooled −4pp; hours −1.3/week (control ≈30). Income elasticity of labor supply ≈ −0.1 to −0.3. Larger hours effect for those without a bachelor's degree. Partners also reduce work.
Model
The L2 model made no prediction: the sign depends on \(v''\). The fall in labor supply tells us \(v''<0\): higher \(\pi\) raises the reservation wage. "Leisure is a normal good."

(c) Labor supply shocks, native wages, and local employment adjustment (Czech commuters)

DiD
Setting
Germany after the fall of the Iron Curtain. From 1991 Czech workers could commute to work in specific German districts along the border but could not live there. Czech employment share in border municipalities rose to ≈3% on average by 1993 (some places far higher), ≈0 inland.
Design
Difference-in-differences: border districts (treated) vs inland districts (control), before vs after 1991, with intensity varying by distance to the border. Key assumption: parallel trends. Event-study pre-trends are flat. Threat: reunification-era shocks hitting the border region differently. Mostly plausible.
Findings
A 1% labor supply shock lowered native wages ≈0.13% and native employment ≈0.93%. The employment fall is mostly reduced inflows of natives into the region rather than layoffs; effects larger for young and low-skilled.
Model
Competitive model: supply shifts out along a demand curve, wages fall. The small wage / large employment response indicates elastic native supply via mobility. Because commuters did not consume locally, there was no offsetting demand shift, unlike ordinary immigration (Card 1990; DSS 2016 JEP: small wage effects).

Lecture 3 · Technological change

(d) Mechanizing agriculture (Karnataka)

RCT
Setting
Rural Karnataka, India. Smallholders (median 2 acres; rice, cotton, maize). Control farmers use ≈89 labor-days per acre, 66 of them hired. Mechanization is still ongoing here, unlike rich countries where it reshaped the whole occupational structure.
Design
RCT: vouchers for renting equipment (cultivators, rotavators), worth ≈1/3 of typical mechanized hours, scaled by farm size; some got part as cash. Assumptions: random assignment (balanced); SUTVA. Because treated farms are small relative to the local labor market this estimates firm-level, partial-equilibrium effects by construction.
Findings
Mechanization index rose ≈0.13–0.16 (highly significant). Labor use per acre fell: family male −16%, hired female −12%, hired male −5% (n.s.). Wages ≈ unchanged. Output and revenue per acre ≈ unchanged; profits possibly up.
Model
Firm-level automation: machines substitute for labor, employment falls. No wage effect is expected in partial equilibrium (aggregate labor demand barely moved). Fills the "Caunedo & Kala" column of the table: productivity ↑, wages ≈0, employment ↓, output ≈0.

(e) Robots and jobs: evidence from US labor markets

Shift-share IV
Setting
US commuting zones, 1990–2007. Industrial robots (automatic, reprogrammable, multipurpose machines), concentrated in automotive, electronics, plastics, metals. Exposure map: Midwest manufacturing belt most exposed.
Design
Two problems: US industry robot data only start 2004, and robot uptake is not random (could respond to demand or labor supply shocks; aging explains Germany/Japan). Solution: measure industry robot growth in Europe ("adjusted penetration" APR: robots per 1990 worker net of output growth), map to commuting zones via 1970 industry employment shares, and use as an instrument for US exposure. Assumptions: relevance (strong: first-stage F ≈ 35); as-good-as-random/exclusion (European industry adoption unrelated to US local shocks other than through US robots; controls for Chinese imports, routine jobs, demographics; no 1970–90 pre-trends); monotonicity. Reasonable.
Findings
Industry level: more robots → lower labor share, higher value added per worker. Commuting zones: one more robot per 1,000 workers → employment-to-population −0.2 to −0.4pp (IV: −0.39), hourly wages −0.4 to −0.8% (IV: −0.77%). Concentrated in manufacturing, routine manual and blue-collar jobs, and workers without college; no effect for graduate degrees.
Model
Equilibrium evidence for the automation model: wages ↓, employment ↓, output/productivity ↑. Opposite sign to labor-augmenting change.

(f) A short-run view of what computers do (UK ICT tax incentive)

RD
Setting
United Kingdom, 2000–2004. A tax policy let firms with 50 or fewer employees immediately deduct ICT investment (computers, software).
Design
Regression discontinuity comparing firms just below and just above the 50-employee cutoff. Assumptions: firms near the cutoff are otherwise similar (continuity); no manipulation (little bunching at 50); the policy was unanticipated; discontinuities appear during the policy window and not before or after. Compelling.
Findings
Eligible firms sharply increased ICT investment. On average, employment/hours and wages rose. But gains accrued to non-routine cognitive workers; routine cognitive workers saw lower earnings and hours.
Model
Average effects fit labor-augmenting change (ICT complements labor); routine-cognitive effects fit automation. Lesson: the same technology can be a complement for some workers and a substitute for others, so "which model" is an empirical, group-specific question. (This was HW1 Q3.)

Lecture 4 · Education

(g) Does compulsory school attendance affect schooling and earnings?

IV
Setting
US men born 1930–1939 (also 1920s and 1940s cohorts), 1980 Census. Interaction of school-entry laws (start in the fall of the year you turn 6) and compulsory attendance until age 16.
Design
IV with quarter of birth as the instrument for years of schooling. Early-year births are older in their grade and can drop out with less schooling (≈9.5 vs 10.5 years in the stylized example). Relevance: Q1-born have ≈0.1 fewer years (clear saw-tooth). As good as random: debated; Bound, Jaeger & Baker (1995) note correlations of QOB with family income and health and weak-instrument bias. Exclusion: QOB must affect earnings only via schooling (age-at-entry effects are a threat). Monotonicity: plausible.
Findings
Q1-born earn less (log weekly wage −0.011) and have less schooling (−0.109 years). Wald/IV return to schooling = 0.102 per year vs OLS 0.071. IV ≥ OLS, so ability bias does not inflate the observed return much. LATE: the return for those on the dropout margin.
Model
Large causal returns ⇒ selection is not the whole story; consistent with human capital. Cannot distinguish human capital from signaling on its own.

(h) The signaling value of a high school diploma

Fuzzy RD
Setting
Texas. Students who failed the high-school exit exam repeatedly reach a "last-chance" exam that decides diploma receipt. Linked to administrative earnings for ≈11 years after.
Design
Fuzzy regression discontinuity at the passing score. Diploma receipt jumps from ≈0.4 to ≈0.9 at the cutoff; passing is an instrument for the diploma, controlling for flexible functions of the score. Assumption: continuity (students can't precisely control their score). The score density is smooth at the cutoff and covariates are balanced. Plausible. The design randomizes the credential while holding learning fixed, which is exactly what a test of signaling requires.
Findings
Diploma receipt has no effect on earnings: ≈ +$100–180/year (1–2%), insignificant; PDV through year 11 ≈ +$600–1,700 (0.8–2%), insignificant. Similar zeros by gender, race, and local mobility.
Model
Rejects signaling for the high-school diploma. Graduates do earn more than non-graduates with the same years, so the diploma correlates with ability, but employers evidently learn productivity from other sources. With A&K, the evidence weighs toward human capital.

Lecture 5 · Compensating differentials

(i) Compensating wage differentials: lessons from the Deadliest Catch

Panel fixed effects
Setting
Alaskan Bering Sea crab fishing, historically the most dangerous task in the most dangerous occupation (≈300 deaths per 100,000 workers in 2006; 80% drowning or hypothermia). Workers earn $20–30k for a 3–4 week season. Panel of workers and vessels across seasons with season-level fatality rates.
Design
Regress log wages on the fatality rate with worker fixed effects, vessel effects, or worker–vessel "match" effects, so identification comes from the same worker facing different risk over time. Controls for wave height, temperature, injury rates and costs. Assumption: worker productivity is fixed over time (so the fixed effect absorbs it) and time variation in risk is unrelated to other unobserved pay determinants. Reasonable.
Findings
Fatality-rate coefficient ≈ 0.029–0.036 on log wages across specifications (one more death per 1,000 FTE raises wages ≈3%). Riskier seasons pay more, within worker. Wave height also carries a premium.
Model
Supports compensating differentials once worker productivity is held fixed, addressing the normal-good/productivity sorting bias from HW2 Q2.

(j) Randomized safety inspections and risk exposure on the job (VSL)

Random assignment → DiD/event study + IV
Setting
US manufacturing plants in federal-OSHA states, 1987–1997, linked to Census of Manufactures data. OSHA randomly selects plants for "programmed" surprise inspections that last days and force correction of violations.
Design
Random inspection is the treatment. Balance table: inspected and non-inspected plants identical on wages, size, productivity, turnover. Event study around inspection (flat pre-trends). Then IV: inspection → fatality rate (first stage) → wages (second stage). Assumptions: random selection (✓), parallel trends (✓), exclusion: inspections change wages only via risk. They check inspections' effects on productivity, capital, and other workers' wages; small effects, but this is the assumption to flag.
Findings
Inspections cut fatalities by ≈1.3 per 10,000 production workers; plant wages fall ≈$0.20–0.35/hour afterward and stay down. Second stage: a 1 per 10,000 higher fatality rate raises wages ≈$0.20/hr. Implied VSL ≈ $6–9 million (e.g. $5.7M–$9.4M across specifications). No spillovers to related plants.
Model
Compensating differentials: a safer job pays less, so there is a trade-off at the margin, and \(VSL=\Delta\text{earnings}/\Delta P(\text{death})\).

(k) Valuing alternative work arrangements

Randomized choice experiment
Setting
(1) ≈7,000 applicants to a real national phone-survey job: each saw two positions identical except for the arrangement and a randomized wage gap, and chose one (incentive-compatible: they were considered for the job they chose). (2) Understanding America Study: hypothetical pairs. Arrangements: flexible schedule, flexible number of hours, work from home, combined flexibility, and "employer discretion" (schedule varies week to week, set a week ahead).
Design
Randomized wage premium → trace the share choosing each job at each premium → the full distribution of willingness to pay, corrected for inattention. Assumptions: real stakes (field) and attention. Avoids the equilibrium problem that market differentials only reveal the marginal worker and the sorting problem that comparable jobs are rare.
Findings
Mean WTP per hour (baseline ≈$16–19): flexible schedule $0.48 (≈2.5% of wage); flexible hours −$0.22 (slightly negative); work from home $1.33 (≈8%); to avoid employer discretion $3.41 (≈20%; 29% in UAS). Distributions are skewed: medians near zero for flexibility, a minority with high WTP. Workers also pay to avoid 50-hour weeks. Maestas et al. (2023): large WTP for PTO, training, avoiding heavy physical work; amenity-adjusted wages narrow the gender gap, widen others.
Model
Fits the compensating differentials framework and explains the small observed market differentials: the marginal applicant has near-zero WTP for flexibility, so firms need not pay for it, even though some workers value it highly. The one amenity with large average WTP (predictable schedules) is where a big differential should, and does, show up.

Homework 1 · Q1: Labor demand with three production functions

For each production function, with output price \(p\), wage \(w\), rental rate \(r\): (i) short-run labor demand with \(K\) fixed; (ii) its elasticity; (iii) long-run labor demand with \(K\) chosen optimally; (iv) its elasticity vs the short run; (v) the effect of \(r\).

HW1 Q1a · Leontief\(Y=A\big(\min\{\alpha L,K\}\big)^\nu\)
Solution

(i) Never choose \(L>K/\alpha\). For \(\alpha L<K\), \(Y=A(\alpha L)^\nu\); FOC \(pA\nu\alpha^\nu L^{\nu-1}=w\) gives \(L=\big(\tfrac{w}{pA\nu\alpha^\nu}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}\). So \[L^d_{SR}=\min\{K/\alpha,\ (pA\nu\alpha^\nu/w)^{1/(1-\nu)}\}\].

(ii) On the interior, \(\log L=\tfrac{1}{1-\nu}\log(pA\nu\alpha^\nu)-\tfrac{1}{1-\nu}\log w\): elasticity \(-\tfrac{1}{1-\nu}\) (downward-sloping for \(\nu<1\)). At the kink \(L=K/\alpha\): elasticity 0.

(iii) Cost minimization sets \(\alpha L=K\). For output \(Y\): \(L=\tfrac1\alpha(Y/A)^{1/\nu}\), \(K=\alpha L\), so \(c(Y)=(\tfrac w\alpha+r)(Y/A)^{1/\nu}\). Set \(p=c'(Y)\) and solve: \[L^d_{LR}=\tfrac1\alpha\big(\tfrac{w+r\alpha}{\alpha p\nu A}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}\].

(iv) \[\log L_{LR}=\log\tfrac1\alpha+\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\log(w+r\alpha)-\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\log(\alpha p\nu A)\]; elasticity \(\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\cdot\tfrac{w}{w+r\alpha}\). Since \(\tfrac{w}{w+r\alpha}\in(0,1)\), long-run demand is less elastic than interior short-run demand (\(\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\)), but more elastic than the kink case (0).

(v) Short run: capital is sunk, \(r\) irrelevant. Long run: \(\partial\log L/\partial r=\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\tfrac{\alpha}{w+r\alpha}<0\): dearer capital lowers labor demand (complements).

HW1 Q1a · Cobb–Douglas\(Y=AL^\alpha K^\nu\)
Solution

(i) \[w=pA\alpha L^{\alpha-1}K^\nu\Rightarrow L^d_{SR}=\big(\tfrac{w}{pA\alpha K^\nu}\big)^{1/(\alpha-1)}\].

(ii) Elasticity \(\tfrac{1}{\alpha-1}=-\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}\).

(iii) \[r=pA\nu L^\alpha K^{\nu-1}\Rightarrow K=\big(\tfrac{r}{pA\nu L^\alpha}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}\]. Substitute: \[L^d_{LR}=\big(pA\alpha(\nu/\alpha r)^\nu\big)^{1/(1-\alpha-\nu)}\,w^{\frac{\nu-1}{1-\alpha-\nu}}\].

(iv) Elasticity \(\tfrac{\nu-1}{1-\alpha-\nu}\). Rewrite the short-run one as \(\tfrac{\nu-1}{1-\alpha-\nu+\alpha\nu}\): identical except for the extra \(\alpha\nu>0\) in the denominator, so short-run demand is less elastic (Le Chatelier: more margins of adjustment in the long run).

(v) \(\log L_{LR}\) contains \(\tfrac{-\nu}{1-\alpha-\nu}\log r\), negative for \(\alpha,\nu\in(0,1)\) with \(\alpha+\nu<1\): higher \(r\) lowers labor demand.

HW1 Q1a · Perfect substitutes\(Y=A(\alpha L+K)^\nu\)
Solution

(i) \[w=pA\alpha\nu(\alpha L+K)^{\nu-1}\Rightarrow L^d_{SR}=\tfrac1\alpha\Big[\big(\tfrac{w}{pA\alpha\nu}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}-K\Big]\].

(ii) Elasticity \[=\tfrac{1}{\alpha L^d_{SR}}\big(\tfrac{w}{pA\alpha\nu}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\], negative for \(\nu<1\).

(iii) \(\alpha\) units of labor replace one unit of capital, so use labor only iff \(w\le\alpha r\). Then \(Y=A(\alpha L)^\nu\) and \(L^D_{LR}=\big(\tfrac{w}{pA\nu\alpha^\nu}\big)^{1/(\nu-1)}\) if \(w<\alpha r\), and \(0\) if \(w>\alpha r\) (indeterminate at equality). This is the automation model's maximum wage in disguise.

(iv) For \(w<\alpha r\) the long-run elasticity is \(\tfrac{1}{\nu-1}\). The short-run elasticity multiplies this by a factor \(>1\), so short-run demand is more elastic here (the opposite of Cobb–Douglas).

(v) With no capital used, \(r\) has no marginal effect; but a fall in \(r\) below \(w/\alpha\) flips the firm into the zero-labor regime.

HW1 Q1bTwo Cobb–Douglas firms with productivities \(A_1,A_2\). What is aggregate long-run labor demand and its elasticity?
Solution

Sum horizontally: \[L^d_{agg}=\Big[\big(pA_1\alpha(\tfrac{\nu}{\alpha r})^\nu\big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\nu}}+\big(pA_2\alpha(\tfrac{\nu}{\alpha r})^\nu\big)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha-\nu}}\Big]w^{\frac{\nu-1}{1-\alpha-\nu}}\]. Taking logs, the elasticity is still \(\tfrac{\nu-1}{1-\alpha-\nu}\): the same for each firm and for the aggregate, because both are the same power of \(w\).

HW1 Q1cWas it misleading to ignore capital in lecture 2?
Solution

Not really. Short run: define \(\tilde y(L)=y(L,\bar K)\). Long run: \(\tilde y(L)=y(L,K^*(L))-rK^*(L)\), a value-added production function with capital chosen optimally. Modelling capital explicitly matters only for learning how elasticities differ between short and long run.

(c) Labor demand and its elasticity. (d) Equilibrium log wage and log employment. (e) Signs of the effects of \(b\), \(p\), \(N\) for \(\lambda>0\), \(\gamma\in(0,1)\).

(a) Employed iff \(\log w-v_i\ge\log b\); \(w^r_i=b\,e^{v_i}\).

Homework 1 · Q3: What computers do (Gaggl & Wright)

HW1 Q3(a) Describe the research design and assess it. (b) Are the results more consistent with labor-augmenting change or automation?
Solution

(a) UK policy let firms with ≤50 employees immediately deduct ICT investment. RD comparing firms just below vs just above 50. Homework 2 · Q1: Education and technological change (CES)

(b) Iff \(\rho>0\): sufficiently substitutable. With poor substitutes, more productive educated workers make manual workers relatively scarce and raise \(w_m\) more.

(c) \[P[\text{school}]=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-a(\log w_e-\log w_m))}\]; \[L^s_e/L^s_m=\exp(a(\log w_e-\log w_m))=(w_e/w_m)^a\].

(d) Substitute into (a): \[\tfrac{w_e}{w_m}=\big(\tfrac{A_e}{A_m}\big)^{\frac{\rho}{1-a(\rho-1)}}\], \[\tfrac{L_e}{L_m}=\big(\tfrac{A_e}{A_m}\big)^{\frac{a\rho}{1-a(\rho-1)}}\].

Q1 · Equilibrium, then automation (25 min)

Setup

A mass \(N\) of workers have reservation wages with CDF \(P[w^r_i\le w]=(w/c)^\lambda\) for \(w\in[0,c]\), \(\lambda>0\). A price-taking firm produces \(y=(AL)^\gamma\), \(\gamma\in(0,1)\), and sells at price 1.

  1. Write labor supply in logs. What is the labor supply elasticity?
  2. Derive inverse labor demand and labor demand in logs. What is the demand elasticity?
  3. Solve for the equilibrium log wage. Show how \(\log w^*\) and \(\log L^*\) respond to \(\log A\), and sign the responses.
  4. Set \(N=c=A=1\), \(\lambda=1\), \(\gamma=\tfrac12\). Compute \(w^*\), \(L^*\), and output \(Y^*\).
  5. Now a machine that perfectly substitutes for effective labor becomes available at cost \(r\) per unit: \(y=(AL+M)^\gamma\). Write the new inverse labor demand curve. For which \(r\) does automation change the equilibrium of part (d)?
  6. With \(r=\tfrac12\), find the new wage, employment, machines used, and output. Compare to (d) and relate to the "theory table".
Solution

(a) \[L^s=N(w/c)^\lambda\Rightarrow\log L^s=\log N+\lambda\log w-\lambda\log c\]. Elasticity \(\lambda\).

(b) \(w=\partial(AL)^\gamma/\partial L=\gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\). So \(\log L^D=\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}\big(\log w-\log\gamma-\gamma\log A\big)\). Elasticity \(\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}<0\).

(c) Equate: \[\log N+\lambda\log w-\lambda\log c=\tfrac{1}{\gamma-1}(\log w-\log\gamma-\gamma\log A)\]. Multiply by \((\gamma-1)\) and collect: \[ \log w^*=\frac{(1-\gamma)\log N+\lambda(\gamma-1)\log c-\gamma\log A-\log\gamma}{\lambda(\gamma-1)-1}. \] The denominator is negative, so \(\partial\log w^*/\partial\log A=\frac{\gamma}{1+\lambda(1-\gamma)}>0\), and via supply \(\partial\log L^*/\partial\log A=\frac{\lambda\gamma}{1+\lambda(1-\gamma)}>0\). Labor-augmenting change raises wages and employment; the more elastic supply (\(\lambda\)), the more of the gain shows up as employment.

(d) Supply \(L=w\); demand \(w=\tfrac12L^{-1/2}\). So \[w=\tfrac12w^{-1/2}\Rightarrow w^{3/2}=\tfrac12\Rightarrow w^*=2^{-2/3}\approx0.63\], \(L^*\approx0.63\), \(Y^*=(L^*)^{1/2}\approx0.79\).

(e) One machine replaces \(1/A\) workers at the same output, so the firm uses machines iff \(w>rA\). Inverse demand becomes \(w^D(L)=\min\{rA,\ \gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\}\): a maximum wage at \(rA\). It changes the equilibrium iff \(rA<w^*\), i.e. \(r<0.63\) here.

(f) \(r=\tfrac12<0.63\), so the cap binds: \(w^{**}=rA=0.5\). Employment from supply: \(L^{**}=0.5\). Output from price = marginal cost with machines: \(Y=(\gamma/r)^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)}=(0.5/0.5)^{1}=1\). Effective input \(AL+M=Y^{1/\gamma}=1\Rightarrow M=0.5\). Compared with (d): wages ↓ (0.63→0.5), employment ↓ (0.63→0.5), output ↑ (0.79→1), average productivity \(Y/L\) ↑ (1.26→2). Exactly the automation column.

Q2 · Education (20 min)

Parts
  1. A worker lives two periods. Uneducated she earns $30,000 in each. Educated she pays $5,000 tuition in period 1 and earns \(y\) in period 2. The interest rate is 50%. For what \(y\) does she get educated? If \(y=90{,}000\), does she? What if the interest rate rises to 100%?
  2. Now continuous schooling, infinite life, no tuition, with \(y_i(S)=\alpha_i(1+S)^\beta\). Derive optimal \(S^*\). Does ability \(\alpha_i\) affect schooling? Is the observed return to schooling biased by ability here? Contrast with the lecture's \(y_i=\alpha_i+\beta S\).
  3. Signaling: \(y_H=100\), \(y_L=60\), \(c_H=25\), \(c_L=50\), types equally likely. Does a separating equilibrium exist? A pooling equilibrium (with the off-path belief that anyone schooled is high-type)? Would banning school be a Pareto improvement? Redo with \(c_H=15\).
Solution

(a) \(NPV_0=30{,}000+30{,}000/1.5=50{,}000\). \(NPV_1=-5{,}000+y/1.5\). Educate iff \(y/1.5>55{,}000\iff y>82{,}500\). With \(y=90{,}000\): \(NPV_1=55{,}000>50{,}000\), yes. At \(r=100\%\): \(NPV_0=45{,}000\), \(NPV_1=-5{,}000+45{,}000=40{,}000\), no. Higher \(r\) makes future earnings worth less relative to foregone current earnings.

(b) \[\max_S\log y(S)-S\log(1+r)\Rightarrow\frac{y'}{y}=\frac{\beta}{1+S}=\log(1+r)\approx r\Rightarrow S^*=\beta/r-1\]. \(\alpha_i\) cancels: ability scales earnings proportionally at every \(S\), so the proportional return is unchanged and everyone picks the same \(S\). Because schooling does not depend on \(\alpha_i\), regressing \(\log y\) on \(\log(1+S)\) across people recovers \(\beta\): no ability bias. In the lecture's additive model, \(S_i^*=1/r-\alpha_i/\beta\): ability lowers schooling and biases the observed return downward. Lesson: ability bias requires ability to change the schooling choice.

(c) \(y_H-y_L=40\). Separating needs \(c_H<40<c_L\): \(25<40<50\) ✓ exists. Pooling needs \(40<2c_H=50\) ✓ exists. Both exist (multiple equilibria). Ban: Pareto-improving iff \(40<\min\{c_L,2c_H\}=50\) ✓: under separating \(H\) gets \(100-25=75\), \(L\) gets 60; under pooling both get 80. Everyone prefers the ban. With \(c_H=15\): separating ✓ (\(15<40<50\)); pooling needs \(40<30\) ✗; ban not Pareto-improving since \(H\) gets \(85>80\) by separating.

Q3 · Compensating differentials (15 min)

Parts

Workers \(i\in[0,1]\) with \(u_i=C-a_ir\), \(a_i\sim U[0,1]\). Healthy industry: \(y_H=A_HL\) with \(A_H=1\). Gross industry: \(y_G=A_GL_G-L_G^2\), risk \(r_G\).

  1. Derive labor supply to \(G\) and inverse labor demand in \(G\).
  2. With \(A_G=3\), \(r_G=1\): find \(w_G^*\), \(L_G^*\), and the compensating differential.
  3. Double the risk to \(r_G=2\). Recompute. Which curve moved and which way?
  4. Back to \(r_G=1\) but \(A_G=3.5\). Recompute. Which curve moved?
  5. Interpret \(r_G\) as the annual probability of death. Show that the differential divided by \(r_G\) equals the marginal worker's \(a_i\), and explain why this is the VSL. If a job pays $2,000/year more and carries a 1-in-5,000 annual fatality risk, what VSL is implied?
  6. Why would comparing average wages of \(G\) and \(H\) workers in survey data be a biased estimate of this differential? Name one paper that addresses the problem and how.
Solution

(a) Choose \(G\) iff \(w_G-a_ir_G\ge A_H\iff a_i\le(w_G-A_H)/r_G\). \(L^s_G=(w_G-1)/r_G\) (for \(w_G\in[1,1+r_G]\)). Demand: \(w_G=y_G'=A_G-2L_G\).

(b) \(L=w-1\), \[w=3-2L\Rightarrow w=3-2(w-1)\Rightarrow w_G^*=5/3\approx1.67\], \(L_G^*=2/3\). Differential \(=2/3\).

(c) \(L=(w-1)/2\), \(w=3-(w-1)\Rightarrow w_G^*=2\), \(L_G^*=1/2\). Differential \(=1\) (↑ from 2/3). Supply shifted left (each worker needs more pay per unit risk), employment fell, wage rose.

(d) \(L=w-1\), \(w=3.5-2(w-1)\Rightarrow w_G^*=5.5/3\approx1.83\), \(L_G^*\approx0.83\). Differential \(\approx0.83\) (↑). Demand shifted up; more workers drawn in; the new marginal worker dislikes risk more.

(e) The marginal worker is indifferent: \(w_G-a_{m}r_G=A_H\Rightarrow a_m=(w_G-A_H)/r_G\): the differential per unit of death probability. That is the marginal worker's dollar value of a unit change in death probability, i.e. \(VSL=\Delta\text{earnings}/\Delta P(\text{death})\). \(2{,}000\times5{,}000=\$10\) million.

(f) Workers differ in productivity, and with \(\gamma<0\) (amenities a normal good) less productive workers sort into \(G\), so \[E[w_H]/E[w_G]=(A_H/A_G)\cdot E[\theta\mid H]/E[\theta\mid G]\]: the raw ratio understates the differential. Lavetti uses worker and worker–vessel fixed effects (within-worker variation in risk). Lee & Taylor use randomly assigned OSHA inspections that change a plant's risk. Mas & Pallais sidestep the market and randomize the wage gap in job offers.

Q4 · Name the design, state the assumption (10 min)

For each scenario: which design, what it estimates, the key assumption, one threat.
  1. In 2010 Oregon raised its compulsory schooling age from 16 to 18. Washington did not. You have earnings for cohorts in both states before and after.
  2. Firms with annual revenue above $5 million must offer paid sick leave. You compare firms with revenue just above and just below $5 million.
  3. You instrument college attendance with distance from the childhood home to the nearest college.
  4. An oversubscribed charter school allocates seats by lottery. You compare lottery winners and losers; 70% of winners enroll.
Solution

(a) Difference-in-differences. Estimates the effect of the law on affected cohorts. Assumption: parallel trends (Oregon and Washington earnings would have moved together); check pre-trends. Threat: Oregon-specific shocks after 2010 (a local recession, other education reforms).

(b) Regression discontinuity with revenue as the running variable. Estimates the local effect at the $5M threshold. Assumption: continuity of potential outcomes at the cutoff. Threat: manipulation: firms shrink or misreport revenue to stay below $5M; check the density for bunching.

(c) Instrumental variables. Estimates a LATE for people whose attendance depends on distance. Assumptions: relevance (distance predicts attendance), as good as random, exclusion (distance affects earnings only via college), monotonicity. Threat: families near colleges live in urban, higher-wage labor markets, violating exclusion/as-good-as-random.

(d) RCT (lottery). Winner-vs-loser comparison is an intention-to-treat effect; dividing by the 70% take-up (lottery as instrument for enrollment) gives the LATE for compliers. Assumptions: the lottery was fair; no spillovers between winners and losers. Threat: differential attrition from the data.

Q5 · Papers and models (15 min, answer two)

Prompts
  1. Acemoglu & Restrepo find that robots reduce wages and employment; Gaggl & Wright find that computers raise them. Is this a contradiction? Use the two models of lecture 3.
  2. Clark & Martorell find a high-school diploma is worth roughly nothing; Angrist & Krueger find a year of schooling is worth about 10%. Reconcile these, and say what each tells you about human capital, signaling and selection.
  3. In the RAND survey, jobs with paid vacation pay $52/hour and jobs without pay $37. Explain, using the HW2 Q2 model and one empirical paper, why this does not refute compensating differentials.
Solution

(a) No. The theory says technology raises wages and employment when it complements labor (labor-augmenting: demand curve \(\gamma A^\gamma L^{\gamma-1}\) shifts up) and lowers them when it substitutes (automation: demand capped at \(rA\)). Which case applies is technology- and worker-specific. Robots are near-perfect substitutes for routine manual tasks, so A&R's equilibrium IV estimates (−0.39pp employment, −0.77% wages per robot per 1,000 workers) fit automation. ICT complements non-routine cognitive work, so Gaggl & Wright's RD finds higher hours and wages on average, while routine cognitive workers, whose tasks ICT substitutes, lose. Both papers are consistent with the same theory applied to different groups.

(b) They answer different questions. A&K estimate the causal return to a year of schooling for near-dropouts using quarter-of-birth IV: ≈0.10, slightly above OLS, so selection/ability bias is not the main story and schooling has real returns. C&M estimate the value of the credential holding learning fixed, via RD at the last-chance exam: ≈0, so employers do not pay for the diploma per se, rejecting signaling for high school. A real return to years with no return to the credential is exactly what human capital predicts and what pure signaling or pure selection would not.

(c) The raw gap compares different workers. In HW2 Q2 with \(\gamma<0\), amenities are a normal good: low-productivity workers sort into the low-amenity ("gross") industry, so \[E[w_H]/E[w_G]=(A_H/A_G)\cdot E[\theta\mid H]/E[\theta\mid G]\] is biased toward "good jobs pay more". Jobs with paid vacation are also better in other unmeasured ways. Lavetti holds the worker fixed (worker and match fixed effects) and finds riskier seasons pay ≈3% more per extra death per 1,000; Lee & Taylor use randomly assigned OSHA inspections and find safer plants pay ≈$0.20–0.35/hr less. Once productivity is controlled, the differential appears.

Q6 · True or false, one line of justification each (5 min)

Statements
  1. In the lecture 2 model, an increase in unearned income reduces labor supply.
  2. In the automation model, introducing machines always lowers the equilibrium wage.
  3. Both labor-augmenting change and automation raise average labor productivity.
  4. A separating equilibrium in the signaling model requires \(c_L>y_H-y_L\).
  5. IV estimates the average treatment effect for the whole population.
  6. The key RD assumption is that the running variable is randomly assigned.
  7. The compensating differential equals the average worker's willingness to pay for the amenity.

  9. In the competitive model, immigration must lower native wages.
  10. Paycheck Plus implies an extensive-margin labor supply elasticity close to 1.

  3. True. Augmenting: \(y/L=w/\gamma\) rises with \(w\). Automation: output up, employment down.

Designs

RCT: ATE ≈ mean(T) − mean(C). Random assignment, no spillovers, ITT if partial take-up.

IV: \(\beta=\delta/\pi\) = reduced form / first stage. Relevance, as-good-as-random, exclusion, monotonicity → LATE for compliers.

RD: \(D=1\{X\ge c\}\); \(E[\nu\mid X]\) continuous at \(c\); \(\beta=\lim_{c^+}E[Y|X]-\lim_{c^-}E[Y|X]\). Check density (no bunching). Fuzzy = IV with cutoff.

DiD: \((Y_{T,1}-Y_{T,0})-(Y_{C,1}-Y_{C,0})\); \(Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\delta_t+\beta T_{it}+u_{it}\); parallel trends; event study with \(\beta_{-1}=0\), pre-periods as placebos.
