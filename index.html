Learn it from zero, in nine lessons

You do not need all of Econ 155A. You need to recognise about 30 paper findings (the true/false section) and tell three graph stories (the short answers). These lessons teach exactly that, in the order that scores. Each lesson is 25–40 minutes: read the idea, look at the picture, then answer the "say it back" questions out loud before opening the answers. Tick the lesson when you can answer them without looking.

Lesson 1 · How this exam works, and how to write answers (10 min)

  • Format. About 8 true/false statements, each needing 1–2 sentences of justification, then 3 short answers that each want a graph plus a paragraph. The T/F items are almost all "paper X found Y" or a lecture fact. The short answers are three graph stories that you can learn by heart.
  • T/F recipe. Verdict, then one sentence naming the paper and the direction of the finding. Example: "False. Baum-Snow uses the 1947 planned highway map as an instrument and finds that highways caused suburbanization, not the reverse." Never leave a T/F blank; a verdict with a reason gets partial credit even if wrong.
  • Short answer recipe. (1) Draw axes and label them. (2) Draw the starting curves and mark the starting point. (3) Draw the shift(s) and mark the new point. (4) Write the mechanism in the order the shifts happen. (5) Say what is certain and what is ambiguous. (6) One sentence citing a paper.
  • Vocabulary you need, in plain words. Equilibrium: where the two curves cross; nobody wants to change what they are doing. Shift right/left: at every price, more/less quantity. Elastic: quantity responds a lot to price (flat curve); inelastic: it barely responds (steep curve). Deadweight loss: value destroyed that nobody receives. Amenity: something nice about a place (clean air, safety); disamenity: the opposite. Rent here means the price of land per period. Bid rent: the most someone would pay for a piece of land at a given location.
Say it back
  1. What two kinds of questions are on this exam? Paper-finding T/F, and graph-based short answers.
  2. What are the six steps of a short answer? Axes, starting curves, shifts, mechanism in order, certain vs ambiguous, one citation.

Lesson 2 · Why cities exist (30 min)

The idea in five sentences. If everyone were equally productive and there were no economies of scale, nobody would bother to trade or crowd together, so there would be no cities. Cities appear when it pays to produce in one place: a factory makes shirts six times faster than home production, and workers cluster near it to save on commuting, bidding up land. Whether production concentrates in one place depends on a race between economies of scale (one big factory is cheaper per unit) and transport costs (shipping to everyone else). When transport costs fall (railroads, trucks), concentration wins. Beyond factories, firms cluster because being near other firms makes them more productive: that is agglomeration.

The four reasons firms cluster (memorise these). Sharing intermediate inputs (dressmakers share a button maker; Apple could not find screws in Texas). Sharing a labor pool (a firm whose demand swings can hire more in good times and fewer in bad times without moving the wage). Better matching of workers to jobs (more firms, closer fit, less training). Knowledge spillovers ("mysteries of trade are in the air"; patents cite nearby patents). Same-industry clustering is called localization economies; mixed-industry clustering is urbanization economies.

The core-periphery rule. Five islands. Produce everything in one core if the saved fixed costs beat the shipping cost: \(C(N)+\tfrac45NT < 5\,C(N/5)\). The threshold transport cost \(T^*\) is bigger when scale economies are bigger. That is the whole of Krugman you need.

Three papers that go with this lesson. Bleakley and Lin: towns founded at river portage points are still big today, long after portage stopped mattering, so once a city exists, agglomeration keeps it there. Greenstone, Hornbeck, Moretti: when a county wins a huge new plant, the existing plants nearby become about 12% more productive within five years compared with the runner-up county; spillovers are real. Ellison and Glaeser: their index measures whether an industry is more clustered than random dart throws would make it; most industries are, some extremely (wine, fur).

Say it back
  1. What two forces decide whether production concentrates? Scale economies vs transport costs; falling transport costs favor concentration.
  2. Name the four agglomeration mechanisms. Inputs, labor pool, matching, knowledge.
  3. Bleakley-Lin: true or false, portage cities declined? False, they stayed dense: path dependence.
  4. Why does a cluster raise a firm's expected profit (labor pooling)? It can expand when demand is high and shrink when low at a stable wage; the gains in good times outweigh the losses in bad times.

Lesson 3 · The urban labor market graph and the pollution tax story (35 min)

The picture. Wage on the vertical axis, employment (which is also city size) on the horizontal. Labor demand slopes down: firms hire fewer workers when wages are high. Labor supply slopes up: a higher wage pulls migrants into the city. The crossing is the city's wage and size.

What shifts each curve. Demand shifts right when firms want more workers: more demand for the city's exports, higher productivity, lower business taxes, better services for firms. Supply shifts right when the city becomes a nicer place to live at any wage: cleaner air, less crime, lower residential taxes, better schools and parks.

The pollution tax story (practice exam SA 1, learn it cold). A tax on pollution raises firms' costs, so demand shifts left. But the air gets cleaner, which is an amenity, so supply shifts right. Both shifts push the wage down. Employment goes down from the first shift and up from the second, so city size is ambiguous. Within the city, the dirty industry shrinks (its lower wage is offset by the tax) and the clean industry grows (it enjoys the lower wage with no tax). Then cite: Greenstone and Gallagher found Superfund cleanup had no measurable effect on nearby house prices at the neighborhood (tract) level; Gamper-Rabindran and Timmins found real but very local effects, +19% within 1 km. So the amenity pull is probably modest.

Papers for this lesson. Suarez-Serrato and Zidar: cutting state business taxes raises the number of businesses, population, wages and rents (demand shifts right). Moretti 2004: a city with more college graduates pays higher wages to everyone, including non-graduates. Moretti 2012: educated cities are pulling away from the rest ("Great Divergence"), and because wages there are rising, it is firms' demand for graduates, not just nice amenities, that drives it.

Say it back
  1. List two things that shift supply right and two that shift demand right. Supply: amenities, less crime, lower residential tax. Demand: export demand, productivity, lower business tax.
  2. Pollution tax: which curves move, which way, and what happens to wage and size? D left, S right; wage falls; size ambiguous.
  3. Superfund: what did the two papers find? GG: nothing at tract level. GRT: big but local, +19% within 1 km.

Drill: "Which curve shifts" (L4 group), until green. Then Practice exam SA 1.

Lesson 4 · How big should a city be, and should the government push places? (40 min)

Utility curves. Plot a worker's well-being against the share of people living in city A. It rises at first (agglomeration) then falls (congestion, long commutes). With two cities, people move until well-being is equal in both: the crossing of A's curve and B's curve (B's read right to left). If both curves slope down at the crossing, a few people moving makes the crowded city worse and the other city better, so they move back: stable. If one curve slopes up, movement feeds on itself: unstable. Migration makes well-being equal; it does not make total well-being as large as possible. A planner would move people until the marginal effect on everyone is equal, which happens only if the two cities' curves have the same elasticity. So free migration can leave one city too big.

The benchmark for place-based policy (Rosen-Roback). If workers and firms can move freely and everyone has the same tastes, then well-being is the same everywhere already. Any subsidy to one place just attracts people until the gain is competed away, and the money ends up with landowners. Moving jobs around is useless unless something was already broken: a tax that burdens some places more, missing credit, a minimum wage that blocks hiring, or agglomeration spillovers the market ignores.

The subsidy graph. A $1,000-per-worker wage credit shifts demand up. Jobs rise, the wage rises. The cost is $1,000 × all workers. Part of that goes to firms, part to workers who were already there, part is a real gain to newcomers, and the rest is deadweight loss. The absurd result: the cheapest "job creation" happens when supply is vertical and no jobs are created at all, because then it is a pure transfer with no waste.

Big push. If a region has two possible stable outcomes, a bad one and a good one, a large temporary investment (a dam, a highway system, an airport, high-speed rail) might flip it permanently to the good one. Evidence: Davis and Weinstein found Japanese cities bombed in WWII, even Hiroshima and Nagasaki, returned to their old growth path within about 20 years, which says cities have one natural size, not several. Kline and Moretti on the TVA (dams and electricity in the Tennessee Valley, 1933): manufacturing gains persisted after the federal money stopped but agriculture gains reversed; agglomeration effects are real but not strong enough to create a second equilibrium; the program roughly paid for itself.

Empowerment Zones (Busso, Gregory, Kline). 1994 federal program: tax credits for hiring people who live and work in poor urban zones, plus block grants. Compared with neighborhoods that applied and lost: jobs rose, wages of residents working inside the zone rose about 13%, commuters' wages did not change, rents did not rise, house values rose about 28%, population did not change. Winners: resident workers and property owners. The T/F trap: the wage gains went to residents, not commuters.

Say it back
  1. What makes a two-city equilibrium stable? Both utility curves sloping down at the crossing, so a small migration reverses itself.
  2. Why is a local subsidy wasteful in the Roback benchmark? Mobile workers compete away gains; incidence falls on landowners; cost exceeds the sum of benefits unless there is a prior distortion.
  3. Davis-Weinstein and TVA: do they support multiple equilibria? No. Cities returned to trend; TVA shows persistence from slow adjustment, not a new equilibrium.
  4. EZ: whose wages rose? Zone residents working in the zone, about 13%; not commuters.

Optional drills: Two-city equilibrium; Local wage credit; HW2 tax.

Lesson 5 · Bid rent and the streetcar story (35 min)

Bid rent in one sentence. The most a user will pay for land at a location is what is left over after paying everything else, so land near the center, where commuting or shipping is cheap, commands higher rent. Plot rent against distance from the center: it falls as you move out. Firms (offices) have the steepest curve because face-to-face contact is worth the most to them, so they win the center; households are next; farms are last. Whoever bids most at each distance gets the land, which is why cities have a business center, a residential ring, and farms outside.

The slope. If every household uses \(h\) square feet and a mile of commuting costs \(t\) dollars, then moving one mile out must save \(t\) dollars of housing: the price per square foot falls by \(t/h\) per mile. With \(t=\$50\) and \(h=1{,}000\) sq ft that is 5 cents per square foot per mile. When households can choose how much space to buy, they buy less near the center where it is dear and more at the edge where it is cheap, so the curve is steep near the center and flat at the edge: convex. (HW2 Q3 derives this: with log utility for land the rent curve becomes an exponential, and its slope is commuting cost divided by lot size, \(r'(d)=-p/L^*\).)

The streetcar story (practice exam SA 2, learn it cold). Two linked pictures: the land market (rent vs distance, with a business curve and a residential curve) and the labor market (wage vs employment). Step 1: the streetcar cuts the cost of a mile of commuting, so the residential curve becomes flatter and reaches farther out; the city's residential area grows, so more people live there: labor supply shifts right. Step 2: more workers push the wage down. Step 3: the lower wage means residents have less to spend, so the residential curve drops a little, and it means firms' costs are lower, so firms can bid more for central land: the business curve rises and the business district expands, raising labor demand. End state: a geographically bigger city, a larger workforce, and a lower wage. Common mistake: saying wages rise.

Papers for this lesson. Baum-Snow: did highways cause suburbs or did suburbs cause highways? He uses the 1947 national highway plan (drawn for defense and trade, not for local demand) as an instrument and finds each highway ray through a city cut its central population about 12%; highways explain roughly a third of central-city decline. Glaeser, Kahn and Rappaport: the poor live downtown mainly because of public transit (they can avoid owning a car), not because the rich want more land; in old cities with subways the poor cluster near the center.

Say it back
  1. Why is the office bid-rent curve the steepest? Face-to-face contact makes central access most valuable to offices.
  2. What is the slope of the housing-price curve and why is it convex? −t/h; households buy less space where it is expensive, so h rises with distance and the slope flattens.
  3. Streetcar: order of effects and the end result? Residential curve flattens → supply right → wage down → business curve up, CBD expands → bigger city, more workers, lower wage.
  4. Baum-Snow: what is the instrument and the finding? 1947 planned rays; highways caused suburbanization, about a third of the decline.

Then Practice exam SA 2 from memory. Optional drills: Housing-price curve; HW2 bid rent.

Lesson 6 · Housing is a weird good: hedonics, filtering, building bans (25 min)

Three ways housing differs. Every unit is different (so prices are really prices of bundles of features), it lasts for decades (so supply responds slowly and cannot shrink quickly), and moving is costly (so people put up with the wrong house for a while).

Hedonic pricing. Regress price on features to get an implicit price for each feature: an extra bathroom, a shorter commute, a nearby toxic site. The weakness is omitted variables: mansions have granite counters and many bathrooms, so the regression credits bathrooms for the counters. Davis (2004) used a sudden childhood leukemia cluster in Churchill County, Nevada: house prices fell about 15% relative to a neighboring county, and dividing the price drop by the increase in risk gives a "value of a statistical life" of $3M–$9M per child.

Filtering. New houses are built at the top of the quality ladder; as they age they slide down and are occupied by progressively lower-income households. That is how most low-income housing is produced. So a ban on new construction hurts everyone, not just the rich: fewer new units means fewer used units trickle down, so prices rise in every quality tier, and some households end up in lower quality than they would have chosen.

Durability and the kinked supply curve (Glaeser and Gyourko 2005). Above the cost of building, supply is elastic: demand goes up, builders build, prices barely rise. Below construction cost, supply is vertical: nobody demolishes houses, so if demand falls, prices crash and the stock stays. Hence declining cities (Detroit) have houses priced below what it would cost to build them. Population losses hit prices hard and persist; population gains hit prices little and are transitory.

Say it back
  1. What is the main problem with hedonic regressions? Omitted variables correlated with measured features.
  2. Davis 2004: what event, what result? Churchill County leukemia cluster; prices fell ~15%; VSL $3–9M per child.
  3. Why does a building ban raise prices for the poor? Filtering: fewer new units at the top means fewer used units trickle down.
  4. Glaeser-Gyourko: what happens to price and quantity after a negative shock? Price falls a lot, quantity does not change; decline persists.

Drill: Glaeser-Gyourko shocks. Optional: Hedonic VSL.

Lesson 7 · Housing policy: vouchers, MTO, the mortgage deduction, rent control, homelessness (45 min)

The menu, cheapest to dearest per dollar of benefit. Vouchers give a poor household money that must be spent on rent (face value = fair market rent − 30% of income); the household picks the unit, so each dollar is worth about a dollar to them. Public housing builds and runs units; it is an in-kind gift, costs about twice what a private unit costs, and the tenant values it at two thirds of market: about 24 cents of benefit per dollar. LIHTC gives developers tax credits to build affordable units; Baum-Snow and Marion show it adds units but crowds out some private building nearby (about 20%, more in gentrifying areas, none in low-demand areas).

Do vouchers push up everyone's rent? Susin said yes, 16% for low-income renters, a net loss to the poor; Eriksen and Ross looked at a huge later expansion and found no effect, so Susin was probably confounded (vouchers go where rents are already high).

The voucher graph (practice exam SA 3, learn it cold). Two cities A (good for kids) and B. Three panels, all with the relative price of A on the vertical axis: demand for A by people from A, demand for A by people from B (lower, they like B), and the total (add them sideways), against the supply of housing in A. Giving B residents vouchers usable only in A shifts panel 2 out, which shifts the total out. The relative price of A rises along the supply curve. Who moves: B residents with vouchers go to A; the A residents who cared least about A are priced out and go to B. Prices: A up, B down. One sentence of caveat: if the point was to get kids out of B, the A-origin families now sent to B partly undo it.

Moving to Opportunity (the write-up part of SA 3). A real experiment, 1994–98, five cities. Families in public housing were randomly offered one of three things: a voucher usable only in a low-poverty neighborhood (plus counseling), a regular voucher, or nothing. Results after 5 years (Kling, Liebman, Katz): movers lived in safer, less poor neighborhoods; adults' mental health improved but their jobs and earnings did not change; girls did better, boys did not. Long-run (Chetty, Hendren, Katz): children who moved before age 13 earned about 31% more as adults; those who moved after 13 did slightly worse (disruption). Lesson: neighborhoods matter for kids, and you have to move early. Related: Chyn found kids displaced by Chicago public-housing demolitions did better too, even teenagers.

Mortgage interest deduction. Owners deduct mortgage interest from taxable income, so the subsidy per dollar of interest equals your tax bracket: a 37% household gets 37 cents, a 15% household gets 15 cents. It costs $66B a year, about two thirds goes to households above $100k, and it tilts people toward owning. T/F trap: it does not give everyone the same subsidy.

Rent control. A price cap below market: fewer units offered, a queue, and lower maintenance. Glaeser and Luttmer add that the scarce units go to whoever got there first, not to who values them most, and that misallocation (about 21% of NYC apartments are the "wrong" size for their occupants) wastes as much as the textbook shortage. Autor, Palmer, Pathak: when Cambridge, MA ended rent control in 1995, decontrolled buildings rose in value and so did the never-controlled buildings next to them (spillovers). Diamond, McQuade, Qian: when San Francisco extended control to small buildings in 1994, protected tenants stayed longer, but landlords converted 15% of units out of the rental market, pushing citywide rents up; today's tenants gain, future tenants lose.

Homelessness facts for T/F. The count is a one-night census each January. The sheltered count fell from 2015 through 2021 (shelters cut capacity in the pandemic) and has risen since; the total hit a record 771,000 in 2024. Studies (Honig and Filer; Quigley et al) find homelessness tracks rents and vacancy rates, and that closing mental hospitals explains surprisingly little. von Wachter et al show it can be predicted months ahead from people's use of county services.

Say it back
  1. Bang per buck: vouchers vs public housing? ≈1 vs 0.24.
  2. Voucher graph: what shifts, what happens to prices, who moves? B-origin demand for A shifts out; P_A/P_B rises, P_B falls; voucher holders B→A, marginal A-origin A→B.
  3. MTO: three groups and the adult vs child results? Low-poverty voucher, regular voucher, control; adults mental health only; kids who moved before 13 gain.
  4. Homeless sheltered count 2015–2021: up or down? Down, then up after 2021.
  5. Who gets the biggest mortgage subsidy? Higher brackets; subsidy rate equals the marginal tax rate.

Then Practice exam SA 3 from memory. Drills: HMID; Voucher face value.

Lesson 8 · Reading a city from its wages and rents (Rosen-Roback) plus real estate papers (30 min)

The idea. People move to wherever they are best off, and firms move to wherever they make normal profit, so in the end nobody can gain by moving. That means a city's wage and rent together reveal its amenities. Draw rent (vertical) against wage (horizontal). Workers' curve slopes up: if rent is higher, they need a higher wage to be just as happy. Firms' curve slopes down: if rent is higher, they can only afford a lower wage. The crossing is the city.

The two cases to memorise. An amenity people like but firms do not care about (clean air, ocean views) shifts the workers' curve: people accept a lower wage to live there, so wages fall for sure; rents could go either way. An amenity that lowers firms' costs (a port, safety) shifts the firms' curve: rents rise for sure; wages could go either way. So: high rents and low wages = a nice place to live (San Diego); low rents and high wages = a productive but unpleasant place (smog, railroads); high rents and high wages = both nice and productive (San Francisco).

Real estate papers (one line each, these are T/F bait). Levitt and Syverson: real estate agents selling their own homes get 3.7% more and wait 9.5 days longer than for clients: they push clients to sell fast. Hsieh and Moretti: with commissions fixed at 6%, higher house prices just attract more agents who each sell fewer houses; earnings per agent stay flat and the extra commission is wasted. Campbell, Giglio, Pathak: foreclosed homes sell about 27% below value and drag down neighbors within a tenth of a mile by about 1% each. Mian and Sufi: the subprime boom was a credit-supply wave: lending surged in ZIP codes where incomes were falling, and defaults followed two years later.

Say it back
  1. Which way do the two curves slope and why? Workers up (higher rent needs higher wage); firms down (higher rent affords lower wage).
  2. Consumption amenity: what is certain? Productive amenity: what is certain? Wage falls; rent rises.
  3. A city with high rents and low wages is…? A place households like more than firms do.
  4. Levitt-Syverson in one line? Agents' own homes sell for 3.7% more and take 9.5 days longer.

Drill: Roback signs, until automatic.

Lesson 9 · Zoning and segregation in 25 minutes

Zoning. Towns use minimum lot sizes to keep out households that would pay less in property tax than they cost in services; the rule of thumb is lot size = target house value ÷ (5 × land price per acre). A growth boundary that stops building beyond 8 miles pushes everyone inside: rents rise inside, fall just outside, inside landowners win, renters everywhere lose. Evidence: regulated places have much higher prices (Katz and Rosen for the Bay Area; Glaeser, Gyourko, Saks show Manhattan prices rose as permits collapsed, not because building got costlier).

Why neighborhoods sort by income. People pay extra to have richer, more educated neighbors (better peers and schools, less crime). Whether that produces segregation depends on who values it more: if the rich value rich neighbors more per unit of land, they outbid and segregation results; but because the rich buy bigger lots, their premium per unit of land is lower, so two poor households can outbid one rich one and integration results. Minimum lot sizes force the poor onto big lots and flip the outcome to segregation.

Measuring and explaining racial segregation. The dissimilarity index \(D=\tfrac12\sum_i|b_i/B-w_i/W|\) is the share of one group that would have to move for every neighborhood to match the city average; US Black-white average 0.73 in 1980, 0.55 in 2020. Cutler, Glaeser, Vigdor: segregation peaked around 1970 and has fallen since; it was first built by collective action (restrictive covenants, violence) and later maintained by whites outbidding Blacks for white neighborhoods. Card, Mas, Rothstein: neighborhoods "tip": once the minority share passes a city-specific point, whites leave in a rush; the tipping point is lower where surveys show less tolerant attitudes. Cutler and Glaeser: Black residents of more segregated cities do worse on schooling, work and single parenthood, and the result survives instrumenting for segregation.

Say it back
  1. Growth boundary: rents inside and outside, who wins? Up inside, down outside; inside landowners win; renters lose.
  2. Why can lot size produce integration, and what does minimum lot size do? Rich pay less per unit of land, so poor pairs outbid them; MLS reverses it.
  3. CGV: when did segregation peak and what changed its cause? 1970; from collective racism to decentralized outbidding.
  4. Card-Mas-Rothstein in one line? Neighborhoods tip past a city-specific minority share, lower where attitudes are less tolerant.

Drills: Lot size and integration; Dissimilarity index.

Part 1 · True/False with 1–2 sentence explanation

1. Bleakley and Lin (2012) find that cities located at historical portage sites declined substantially once portage became technologically obsolete.
Answer

False. Portage sites on the fall line remain unusually dense today even though the original natural advantage disappeared in the 19th century. The persistence is consistent with increasing returns (agglomeration) sustaining activity after the founding advantage is gone: path dependence.

Why it matters: this is the lecture 1 "natural advantage vs. increasing returns" distinction. Natural advantage explains where the city started; increasing returns explain why it stayed.

2. Baum-Snow (2007) finds that increasing suburbanization leads to the construction of interstate highways.
Answer

False. Causality runs the other way: highways caused suburbanization. Baum-Snow instruments actual highway rays with the rays in the 1947 national plan (drawn for trade and defense, not local demand) and finds each extra ray through a central city cut its population by about 12 percent by 1990; highways account for roughly one third of the decline in central city population relative to metro areas.

Add the headline fact if you have room: holding 1950 geography constant, central city population fell 17 percent while MSA population rose 72 percent, 1950–1990.

3. The national count of homeless in shelters was increasing from 2015 to 2021, especially in 2021 during the COVID pandemic, and then has been decreasing since 2021.
Answer

False. The Point-in-Time count of sheltered homeless was falling from 2015 through 2021 (including 2021, when shelters cut capacity during the pandemic) and has been rising since 2021. Lecture 9, 2024 AHAR.

Numbers if you want them: all homeless ≈ 565k (2015) → 580k (2020) → 771k (2024). Sheltered ≈ 392k (2015) → 326k (2021) → 497k (2024). Unsheltered rose nearly every year, 2015 ≈ 174k → 2024 ≈ 274k. The 2021 PIT did not count unsheltered people.

4. The Home Mortgage Interest Deduction provides the same subsidy to homeowners regardless of their marginal income tax rate.
Answer

False. Interest is deducted from taxable income, so the subsidy rate equals the household's marginal tax rate; higher-income households in higher brackets (and with bigger mortgages) get a larger subsidy. About two thirds of the benefit goes to households with income above $100,000.

Lecture 9 example: $100,000 house, 8% interest, $8,000 interest deducted at a 28% marginal rate saves $2,240 a year, which tilts the rent-vs-own choice toward owning. The distortion could be undone by taxing imputed rental income, or more simply by eliminating the deduction.

5. The wage gains from Empowerment Zones were shown to be highest for workers commuting into the zone.
Answer

False. Busso, Gregory and Kline (2013) find the largest wage gains for zone residents working inside the zone (log weekly wage +0.13); commuters into the zone saw essentially no wage change (+0.005). Jobs went to a mix of residents and commuters, rents did not rise, housing values rose about 28 percent.

6. Suarez-Serrato and Zidar (2014) find that cutting state business taxes increases the number of businesses in the affected area.
Answer

True. Lower state business taxes raise the number of establishments (10-year log change slope ≈ 3.9 on the net-of-tax rate), and also raise population, wages and rents. Lecture 4 framed this as taxes shifting the urban labor demand curve.

7. Superfunds (for cleanup of hazardous waste) were found to have negligible effects on housing values.
Answer

Either, if you cite the right paper. True per Greenstone and Gallagher (2008): comparing sites just above and below the federal cleanup score threshold, cleanup had no detectable effect on housing values, rents, population or population composition in the surrounding tracts. False per Gamper-Rabindran and Timmins (2011): redoing the analysis at the Census block level, cleanup raised values 19 percent within 1 km and 5.8 percent within 3 km, with bigger effects on cheaper houses. The effects are real but highly localized, so tract-level averages miss them.

Best answer names both and reconciles them with the "localized within 3 km" point.

8. A "big push" via government intervention may be necessary for large infrastructure projects. Provide a modern example of a project that would require a big push.
Answer

Example answer. A new airport or California high-speed rail. Private markets under-provide lumpy infrastructure because the benefits are hard to capture privately and the capital requirement is enormous; a large one-time public investment can move a region to a new (better) stable equilibrium. Lecture 5 examples: railroads, federal highways, the Hoover dam, the TVA.

If asked for evidence: Kline-Moretti TVA (manufacturing gains persisted after funding stopped in 1960, agriculture gains reversed; agglomeration economies real but not strong enough for multiple equilibria) and Davis-Weinstein (Japanese cities returned to trend after WWII bombing, favoring a single equilibrium).

Part 2 · Short answer

SA 1Discuss, with reference to a graph and the literature, the effect of a pollution tax on wage and city size.
Model answer
  1. Set-up (lecture 4 urban labor market). Upward-sloping city labor supply (migration responds to wages and amenities), downward-sloping city labor demand (substitution and output effects).
  2. Demand side. The pollution tax raises the production cost of the polluting good, raising its price and cutting output and labor demand: D shifts left.
  3. Supply side. The improvement in environmental quality is an amenity. Workers accept a lower wage to live there, so S shifts right.
  4. Result. Wage falls unambiguously. Total employment is logically indeterminate: it falls if the demand shift dominates, rises if the amenity pull dominates.
  5. Distribution across industries. Both the polluting ("steel") and clean industries face the lower wage. In steel the lower wage is offset by the tax so employment falls; in the clean industry the lower wage raises employment.
  6. Roback framing (lecture 11), optional extra credit. Clean air is an unproductive amenity (households value it, firms do not). In Rosen-Roback, dw/dA < 0 for sure, dr/dA ambiguous. Same wage conclusion.
  7. Literature. How much do people value the cleanup? Greenstone and Gallagher (2008): Superfund cleanup, no detectable effect on housing values, rents, population or composition at the tract level (small supply shift). Gamper-Rabindran and Timmins (2011): at the block level, +19% values within 1 km, +5.8% within 3 km, larger for cheaper houses. The amenity effect is real but localized, so the supply shift is probably modest and the demand-side job loss likely dominates for city size.
SA 2Describe the effects of improved residential transportation (e.g., the introduction of streetcars) on land and labor markets. Assume no consumer or input substitution and a rectangular city.
Model answer
  1. Land market, first round. Lower commute cost per mile tilts the residential bid-rent curve outward (flatter slope, since slope = −t/h). The residential territory expands, so more workers live in the city: labor supply shifts right.
  2. Labor market. Excess supply of labor at the old wage pushes the wage down. The lower wage reduces quantity supplied a bit and raises quantity demanded along the demand curve.
  3. Land market, second round. Lower resident income shifts the residential bid-rent curve down, shrinking residential area and labor supply slightly. Lower wages are lower production costs, so the business bid-rent curve shifts up: firms outbid residents for more central land, the business district expands, labor demand rises.
  4. New equilibrium. City is geographically larger (O'Sullivan's example: CBD expands 50 percent, residential area too), workforce is larger, wage is lower (example: $7/hr). With variable density, rents rising near the center raise density and reinforce the territory changes.
  5. Literature hook. Lecture 7: omnibus (1827), cable car (1873), electric trolley (1886), subway (1895) raised the feasible radius and created the hub-and-spoke monocentric city; later the truck (1910) and highways decentralized it. Baum-Snow (2007) for the highway era.
SA 3Two cities A and B, heterogeneous preferences for each. Kids do worse in B, so the planner gives some B residents a voucher usable only for housing in A. Show what happens on your graphs. What happens to prices in A? In B? Who moves where? Then: describe Moving to Opportunity, its intention, structure, and outcomes.
Model answer
  1. Initial equilibrium. Draw demand for A from A-origin residents (panel 1), from B-origin residents (panel 2, lower: they like B more), and the horizontal sum (panel 3) against the supply of housing in A. At high relative prices only A-origin residents demand A, so total demand coincides with panel 1 above the kink.
  2. Voucher. It raises B-origin residents' willingness to pay for A: D_B shifts out, so total demand shifts out in parallel below the kink.
  3. Prices. P_A/P_B rises. Since the voucher moves demand from B to A, the price of housing in B falls (and P_A rises in levels).
  4. Who moves. B → A: voucher recipients, specifically those whose preference gap for B is smaller than the voucher's value. A → B: the A-origin residents with the weakest attachment to A, who at the new higher relative price prefer B. Be precise: the marginal movers in each group are the ones closest to indifference.
  5. Caveat worth one sentence. If the kids' disadvantage in B is a neighborhood effect, the A-origin movers' children are now exposed to B, which partly offsets the program's aim. Also Susin (2003) vs Eriksen-Ross (2015) on whether vouchers raise rents for non-recipients.

Moving to Opportunity

  • Intention. Test whether moving poor families out of high-poverty public housing into low-poverty neighborhoods improves outcomes: a randomized test of neighborhood effects and of the spatial mismatch idea (lectures 9 and 15).
  • Structure. 1994–98, five cities (Baltimore, Boston, Chicago, LA, New York). Families in public housing randomly assigned to three groups: an experimental voucher usable only in a census tract with poverty below 10 percent, plus counseling; a traditional Section 8 voucher with no location restriction; and a control group that stayed in public housing. Compliance was partial: about half of the experimental group used the voucher.
  • Outcomes, 5-year follow-up (Kling, Liebman, Katz 2007). Both voucher groups moved to lower-poverty, lower-crime, less segregated neighborhoods with better schools; the restricted voucher group moved to much lower poverty than the unrestricted group. Adults: large gains in mental health, obesity and subjective well-being, but no significant effects on employment, earnings or welfare receipt. Children: girls improved on mental health, risky behavior and schooling; boys showed some negative effects. Children were happier mainly because the risk of victimization fell. The null adult economic effect does not refute spatial mismatch, because MTO moves did not reduce distance to jobs.
  • Long run (Chetty, Hendren, Katz 2016, IRS data). Children who moved before age 13 earned about 31 percent more at age 26, attended college more and lived in better neighborhoods as adults; children who moved at 13 or older did slightly worse, consistent with disruption. Exposure effect: each additional year of childhood in a low-poverty neighborhood is worth roughly $566 of age-26 earnings. Policy lesson: move early enough for the exposure to accumulate.
  • Related. Chyn (2018): Chicago public housing demolitions forced moves with Section 8 vouchers; displaced children earned more, were less likely to be arrested or drop out, including teenagers, perhaps because the demolished projects were especially distressed. HUD's 2018 pilot: many landlords refuse vouchers outright, more so in low-poverty areas.

Lectures 1–3 · Why cities exist, agglomeration, city size

Testable
  • Four reasons cities exist: natural advantage, transport costs with scale economies, increasing returns, spillovers. A "backyard economy" with equal productivity and constant returns has no cities.
  • Core-periphery (Krugman, HW1): produce in one core when transport cost is below a threshold \(T^*\); \(T^*\) rises with scale economies; falling transport costs and growing scale economies both push toward concentration.
  • Market-oriented vs material-oriented firms (weight-gaining vs weight-losing); the median location minimizes transport cost.
  • Four agglomeration mechanisms: shared inputs, labor pooling, matching, knowledge spillovers. Localization (same industry) vs urbanization (mixed) economies.
  • Labor pooling logic (HW1 Q2): a firm in a cluster can expand in good times and shrink in bad times, so expected profit is higher; a local wage that rises with demand damps that gain.
  • Ellison-Glaeser (HW1 Q3): \(G\) measures concentration beyond overall employment; \(\gamma\) removes the part random plant locations would produce; clustering is real and varies by industry.
  • Greenstone-Hornbeck-Moretti: winner vs runner-up counties; incumbents' productivity +12% after five years. Bleakley-Lin: portage towns persist.
  • Utility curves (HW2 Q1): migration equalizes utility; stable when curves slope down at the crossing; the planner equalizes marginal utility, so free migration is optimal only with equal elasticities; cities can be too big.
  • Duranton-Puga nursery cities; Zipf's law holds for metros, not fixed squares (Holmes-Lee); central place theory: a hierarchy of city sizes from scale economies vs per-capita demand.

L1 · Definitions and the backyard economy

Census geography

  • Blocks → block groups → tracts.
  • Urban area: density above 1,000 per sq. mile. Metropolitan area: urban core of 50k+ with tight commuting ties. Micropolitan: core 10k–50k. Principal city: largest municipality in the metro.
  • Incorporated place (Berkeley), consolidated city (SF), Census Designated Place (Castro Valley; no legal status, no elected officials).
  • Local government authority comes from states; municipal boundaries can be arbitrary. CBSAs combine MSAs.

Backyard economy ⇒ no cities

  • Assume: equal productivity of labor and land, constant returns in exchange and production.
  • Then no trade (no gain from specialization, exchange costs time) and no cities (dense living bids up land with no benefit). Uniform land price and density.
  • Drop each assumption in turn to get a city: comparative advantage + scale economies in exchange ⇒ trading city; increasing returns in production ⇒ factory town.

L1 · Trading cities and factory towns

  • Trading cities. Comparative advantage makes trade worthwhile (North pays at most 3 shirts per loaf, South accepts at least 1; price 2). Net gain = gross gain minus transaction time. Scale economies in exchange mean trading firms do it cheaper; their workers cluster near the firm and bid up land: a trading city. Historical: Athens, Constantinople, Aleppo; US cotton-gin and transport-technology cities.
  • Bleakley and Lin (2012). Portage sites on the fall line were founded on a natural advantage that became obsolete; they are still dense today. Increasing returns keep activity in place: path dependence.
  • Factory town. Factory makes 6 shirts/hour vs 1 at home; bread 1 loaf/hour anywhere. Workers indifferent between home and factory: if urban cost of living is 50% higher, urban wage must be 50% higher. Factory price of a shirt = unit cost = 1/3 loaf (zero profit); net price to a consumer = 1/3 loaf + 1/12 loaf per mile walked. Market area is where net factory price beats home production; better transport technology widens it. Workers cluster near the factory to save commuting, bid up land, density rises: a city.
  • Region of factory towns. Entry until zero profit; factories span the region, each location in some market area; complete specialization (urban shirts, rural bread). Rural land rent is higher near cities (lower travel cost); urban wage compensates for urban land prices.
  • Location orientation. Market-oriented firm: output costlier to ship than inputs (weight-, fragility-, bulk-, hazard-gaining: beverages, fresh food, assembly, explosives). Material-oriented: inputs costlier to ship (weight-, fragility-, hazard-losing: sugar from beets, lumber, canning, deodorizing skunks). Transfer-oriented firm minimizes procurement + distribution cost: PC = w_i t_i x, DC = w_o t_o (x_M − x). Principle of median location: transport cost is minimized at the median of destinations.

L1 · Core-periphery model (Krugman 1991)

Five islands, each with population \(N/5\), unit demand. Transport cost \(T\) per unit from core to each periphery island. Cost of producing \(Q\): \(C(Q)\) with scale economies \(C(N)/N < C(N/5)/(N/5)\).

Centralize in one core iff \[C(N)+\tfrac45 N T \;<\; 5\,C(N/5).\] Threshold: \[T^*=-\tfrac54\Big[\tfrac{C(N)}{N}-\tfrac{C(N/5)}{N/5}\Big],\] which is the (positive) average-cost saving scaled by 5/4. \(T^*\) rises with the degree of scale economies. With \(C(Q)=F+cQ\): \(T^*=5F/N\), independent of marginal cost \(c\).

History: falling \(T\) (railroads, trucks) and rising scale economies (division of labor) both make concentration more likely. HW1 Q1 generalizes to \(T(x)=b+c\ln(1+x)\): centralize iff \(b+c\ln(1+\tfrac45N)<4f\); the elasticity of variable transport cost \(x/[(1+x)\ln(1+x)]<1\), so shipping has its own scale economy, which favors centralization. Falling fixed costs \(f,b\) at the same rate as \(c\) leave the comparison \(b+c\ln(\cdot)\) vs \(4f\) scaled equally: no change in the decision. Marginal cost \(a\) drops out with linear costs but not with quadratic \(f+ax^2\) (then centralizing raises production cost, \(aN^2\) vs \(5a(N/5)^2=aN^2/5\)).

L2 · Agglomeration economies

  • Localization economies: same-industry clustering. Urbanization economies: cross-industry clustering. Four micro-foundations: share intermediate inputs, share a labor pool, better worker-task matching, share knowledge.
  • Intermediate inputs. Dressmakers share a button maker (scale economies in buttons, face time needed, rapidly changing products). High-tech firms share component suppliers and testing. Foxconn/Shenzhen and the Mac Pro screw shortage in Austin: the supplier cluster is the asset. Trade-off of joining a cluster: cheaper inputs vs. competition for workers raising labor cost.
  • Labor pooling (O'Sullivan). Firm-level demand varies, industry demand fixed (zero-sum). Locational equilibrium: wage in cluster = expected wage at isolated site ($10). Profit = triangle between MRP and the wage line. Isolated firm expected profit $48; cluster firm (1/2)(147+3) = $75. Intuition: in the cluster the firm adjusts employment, hiring more when demand is high (at an unchanged wage) and fewer when low; the isolated firm cannot. Good news dominates bad news because the firm responds. HW1 Q2 is the formal version: SF has perfectly elastic supply at w = 1; rural Arkansas has \(L^s=\eta w^2\), so a good productivity draw bids up the local wage and damps profits. Expected profit is convex in \(z\), so rises with \(\varepsilon\) in both places, but more in SF.
  • Matching. Workers' skills on a unit circle, firms with skill requirements, training cost closes the gap; 2 workers per firm, monopolistic competition, zero profit. Larger city ⇒ more firms ⇒ closer matches ⇒ lower training cost.
  • Knowledge spillovers. Marshall's "mysteries of trade in the air." Jaffe, Trajtenberg and Henderson (1993): patent citations are disproportionately local relative to a control group of similar patents; localization fades as knowledge diffuses; university patents most fertile.
  • Other benefits of size: joint labor supply (power couples), learning by imitation (migrants' permanent wage gains), social matching, product variety (Thai food in Topeka; Amazon reduces this).
  • Corporate HQs: large cities specialize in management functions, small cities in production.

L2 · Ellison and Glaeser (1997): the dartboard

If an industry has 5 plants, concentration in 5 places is unsurprising (75% of vacuum cleaner employment at 4 plants). Measure concentration in excess of random "darts."

\(x_i\) = region \(i\)'s share of all employment, \(s_i\) = its share of the industry. \[G=\sum_i (s_i-x_i)^2,\qquad H=\sum_j z_j^2 \text{ (plant-size Herfindahl)},\qquad \gamma=\frac{G-(1-\sum_i x_i^2)H}{(1-\sum_i x_i^2)(1-H)}.\] \(H=1\) with one plant, → 0 with many equal plants. \(\gamma\) nets out the lumpiness due to plant size. Findings: clustering is real, varies a lot by industry, most industries are more clustered than random but the \(H\) adjustment is small except for a few (wine, fur goods extremely clustered). HW1 Q3 computes \(G\) from state employment shares.

L2 · Greenstone, Hornbeck and Moretti (2010): million dollar plants

  • Question: do big plants raise incumbents' productivity (spillovers)? Problem: MDPs are not randomly placed.
  • Design: Site Selection magazine reports the winner and runner-up county for each MDP; compare incumbents in winners to those in losers (as good as a coin toss). Event study plus a regression with plant FE, industry × year FE, pair FE, and winner-specific mean shift and trend break at the opening.
  • Findings: incumbent TFP in winning counties rises about 12 percent after five years relative to losers; effect grows over time (knowledge spillovers take time); strongest for plants "closest" to the MDP in worker flows and technology. Wages rise too, so part of the gain is bid away.

L3 · Utility and city size

  • Within a city (O'Sullivan Table 4-3): utility = labor income + rental income − commute cost − rent paid. Differences in commute cost are offset by rent differences, land rent is shared equally (averaging $15), so utility is equal at every distance ($70).
  • System of cities: fixed regional workforce divided among cities (six of 1M, three of 2M, two of 3M). Utility peaks at 2M (point M). O'Sullivan: cities may be too large (self-correcting on the downward slope) but not too small (self-reinforcing on the upward slope, so a region of six small cities is unstable).
  • Two-city model, symmetric: unique stable equilibrium at the 50-50 split. Even though each city would gain from shrinking, the equal split maximizes population-weighted average utility, so it is socially optimal.
  • Asymmetric: three crossings: E1 and E3 unstable (one curve upward sloping), E2 stable. At E2 both elasticities are negative, \(\eta_A<\eta_B<0\). A benevolent dictator could do better by shrinking A and expanding B to the social optimum S, because the equilibrium equates average utilities while the planner equates marginal social utilities \(u_i+p_i u_i'=u_i(1+\eta_i)\). With equal utilities in equilibrium, optimality requires equal elasticities. In lecture 5 the asymmetric case has two stable equilibria separated by a tipping point; the better one justifies a big push.
  • HW2 Q1 numbers: \(u_A=2-p_A,\ u_B=2-2p_B,\ u_C=\tfrac32-p_C\). Equilibrium \(p=(0.6,0.3,0.1)\), \(u=1.4\), stable (all curves downward sloping). Planner: \(2-2p_A=2-4p_B=1.5-2p_C\) ⇒ \(p=(0.5,0.25,0.25)\), aggregate utility 1.4375 > 1.4. Elasticities at equilibrium −0.43, −0.43, −0.07: unequal, so not optimal (C is too small). An amenity improvement in A pulls workers from B and C until utilities re-equalize; C, with the flatter curve, loses twice as many as B.

L3 · Specialized vs diverse cities, Zipf, central place

  • Nursery cities (Duranton and Puga 2001). Firms experiment with prototypes in diverse cities (expensive but no need to move between specialized cities), then relocate production to a specialized city to exploit localization economies. Diversity measured as \(1/H\), \(H=\sum_j s_{ij}^2\). French data, 341 metros: firms, nearly all R&D relocations, move from diverse to more specialized areas. NYC radio industry is the historical example.
  • Local goods and size: goods with per-capita demand large relative to scale economies (pizza, haircuts) are everywhere; goods with small per-capita demand relative to scale economies (opera, brain surgery) are only in big cities. Bigger cities have more variety.
  • Rank-size rule (Zipf): Rank × Pop = constant, or \(\log\text{Rank}=\log C-b\log\text{Pop}\), exact if \(b=1\). Nitsch (2005) median \(b=1.09\); metro areas \(b=1.02\). Largest MSAs are a bit "too small." Gabaix (1999): Zipf emerges if growth rates are independent of size (Gibrat's law). Holmes and Lee (2010): for fixed 6×6-mile squares, \(b<1\); Zipf fits metro populations, not fixed areas, so the sprawl of political boundaries matters for generating Gibrat.
  • Large primate cities: trading and indivisible import/export facilities, neglect of intra-national transport, politics (Ades and Glaeser 1995: dictators buy off the capital's likely rebels).
  • Central place theory (Christaller, Lösch). Uniform density, no shopping externalities, ubiquitous inputs, perfect substitutes, no complementarity. Jewelry (80k people per store: one store, at the center, minimizing travel), books (20k: four stores), pizza (5k: sixteen). Produces a hierarchy: few large cities, many small; consumers travel up to bigger cities, never down or sideways. Big cities have more of everything. Relaxing assumptions: comparison shopping (imperfect substitutes) and one-stop shopping (complements) encourage clustering; per-capita demand differs across goods (NASCAR vs opera).
Traps
  • "Cities are too small" is what O'Sullivan's figure suggests but the two-city model says the stable equilibrium can be inefficient in either direction; the test is equal elasticities, not equal utilities.
  • Ellison-Glaeser: \(G\) alone overstates clustering for industries with few large plants; \(\gamma\) fixes it, and the fix is usually small.
  • Zipf holds for metro populations with elastic boundaries, not for fixed-area counts.
  • Labor pooling raises expected profit because firms can vary employment; it is not about lower average wages.

Lectures 4–5 · Urban growth and place-based policy

Testable
  • Urban labor market: what shifts demand (export demand, productivity, business taxes, services for firms) and supply (amenities, crime, residential taxes, residential services).
  • Pollution tax: demand left, supply right, wage falls, employment ambiguous; dirty industry shrinks, clean grows. Superfund: Greenstone-Gallagher (no tract-level effect) vs Gamper-Rabindran-Timmins (local, +19% within 1 km).
  • Suarez-Serrato-Zidar (tax cuts raise businesses, population, wages, rents); Moretti 2004 (college share raises everyone's wages); Moretti 2012 (divergence is demand-driven). Bartik: taxes matter, more within a metro than between.
  • Place-based vs person-based; Rosen-Roback benchmark (mobile workers and firms, equal utility, incidence on land) means intervention needs a prior distortion: taxes, credit constraints, missing insurance, low migration, public goods, agglomeration externalities, a binding minimum wage.
  • Wage-credit welfare graph (and HW2 Q2 tax): cost exceeds transfers to firms and prior residents plus newcomers' gain; deadweight loss; inelastic supply means no jobs and no waste.
  • Big push and multiple equilibria; Davis-Weinstein (cities return to trend, one equilibrium); Kline-Moretti TVA (manufacturing persists, agriculture reverses, agglomeration without multiple equilibria); Wang (SEZs raise FDI, productivity, wages).
  • Busso-Gregory-Kline Empowerment Zones: jobs up, resident-in-zone wages +13%, commuters flat, rents flat, values +28%, population flat; winners resident workers and owners.

L4 · Sources of growth and the Great Divergence

  • Sources: technological progress, human capital, agglomeration economies, public investment. A region-wide innovation shifts both cities' utility curves up with no migration.
  • Human capital externalities. Moretti (2004): a higher city college share raises wages of everyone, including less-educated workers (labor is complementary across skills); firm-level productivity gains too, strongest among firms that are "economically close." Proximity to star researchers seeds biotech firms.
  • Great Divergence (Moretti 2012). Variation in college share across metros is large and growing since 1980; earnings of college grads grew most in high-college-share metros. Why? Supply story (nice places bid up land, only the educated can afford them) predicts wage cuts; demand story (productive places need graduates) predicts rising earnings, which is what we see, so probably demand. Diamond (2015): supply plus strong agglomeration effects can also keep wages high. Good news for educated areas, bad for the rest. Glaeser, Kallal, Scheinkman and Shleifer (1991): industrial diversity predicts growth. Ganong and Shoag (2017): cross-state income convergence has slowed, and migration to high-income areas is low because housing costs eat the gain for less-skilled movers.
  • Local multiplier. Export jobs create local jobs: Δtotal employment = Δexport employment × multiplier. Problems: it describes a horizontal shift in labor demand, not the equilibrium change; focuses on jobs not income.

L4 · The urban labor market

Demand slopes down and shifts when…

Substitution effect (other inputs for labor) and output effect (higher costs → higher price → less output). Shifts right with: higher export demand, higher productivity, lower business taxes, better public services for firms, land-use policy that accommodates expansion.

Supply slopes up and shifts when…

Migration responds to wages; a growing city must pay more to offset higher living costs. Shifts right with: better amenities (environment), fewer disamenities (crime), lower residential taxes, better residential public services.

  • Taxes (Bartik 1991). Elasticity of business activity to taxes: −0.1 to −0.6 across cities, −1 to −3 within a city. Manufacturing is most sensitive. Taxes fund services, so they shift both curves; a tax increase could even raise employment if the supply shift is big enough. High-service cities grow faster, ceteris paribus; redistributional spending inhibits growth.
  • Suarez-Serrato and Zidar (2014/2016). State business tax cuts raise population (cumulative event study), establishments (slope 3.94), wages (1.78) and rents (2.16) over ten years.
  • Environmental policy. Pollution tax raises costs ⇒ demand left; cleaner environment ⇒ supply right. Wages fall for sure; total employment indeterminate. Steel loses jobs (lower wage offset by the tax), the clean industry gains.
  • Superfund. Greenstone and Gallagher (2008): sites above vs below the federal cleanup score threshold; no effect on housing values, rents, population or composition. Gamper-Rabindran and Timmins (2011): at the block level, +19% within 1 km, +5.8% within 3 km, bigger for cheaper houses; effects are real but localized.

L5 · Place-based vs person-based, and the Rosen-Roback benchmark

  • Person-based: Medicare. Place-based: state enterprise zones, federal Empowerment Zones, Opportunity Zones (capital-gains deferral, 10–15% exclusion after 5–7 years, full exclusion of new gains after 10 years), TVA, Appalachian Regional Commission, fiscal equalization, Chinese SEZs. Motives are often unclear: help people, use space better, or bring home the bacon. Glaeser and Gottlieb (2008): the rationale for paying people to stay in weak places is "extremely weak."
  • Rosen-Roback benchmark: perfectly mobile workers and firms, fixed total labor supply, homogeneous tastes ⇒ utility equalized everywhere, zero profits; partial equilibrium with a reference utility; no consumer surplus to workers; incidence falls on landowners. Under these assumptions moving activity around is a bad idea by construction: you need a prior distortion to justify intervention.
  • Lesson. A local subsidy may create jobs, but the dollar cost exceeds the benefit to locals and new arrivals, and firms capture part. The absurd corollary: the most efficient development policy creates no jobs. The implicit assumption behind job-creation programs is that there are too few jobs, i.e., a prior distortion.
  • Binding minimum wage as the prior distortion: employment is inefficiently low; a wage subsidy to firms restores jobs, and now benefit to workers + firms exceeds the dollar cost (the subsidy fills a gap that was already deadweight). HW2 Q2 has the mirror image: a $6,000 per-worker tax with flat demand (zero profit at $28k revenue) and supply S = 4w cuts the wage to $22k and jobs from 112k to 88k; revenue $528M vs cost to workers $600M, DWL $72M; with a $24k minimum wage plus the tax, no firm can cover $30k, employment goes to zero; statutory incidence (worker vs firm) does not matter.
  • Prior distortions that can justify place-based policy: federal income taxes burden high-wage cities (Albouy 2009: same-skill workers pay up to 27% more federal tax in high-wage cities, so the offsetting policy would subsidize NY and SF); capital constraints (redlining); missing insurance for local shocks (Shiller-Weiss home equity insurance); inefficiently low migration; under-provided local public goods; agglomeration externalities (subsidize where agglomeration forces are strongest, but the government must know where: hard to predict Silicon Valley; Kline-Moretti: subsidizing industries with strong agglomeration like manufacturing at the expense of weak ones like agriculture may be sensible).
  • Chinese SEZs (Wang 2011). Four southern cities in the late 1970s, expanded in the 1980s. Property-rights protection, tax incentives, foreign land purchase. DiD across waves: FDI jumps, productivity rises, wages and employment rise. Pre-existing market failure (no private property) made intervention fertile; some gains may be agglomeration. Lu, Wang and Zhu (2015) for later county-level zones.
  • Multiple equilibria and big push. Lecture 5 two-city graph with two stable equilibria (E2, E4) and a tipping point (E3). Lumpy infrastructure (highways, dams, airports, high-speed rail) is under-provided privately; a large temporary intervention can knock a region out of a poverty trap into the better equilibrium permanently. Expectations matter: self-fulfilling coordination justifies advertising and seed money.
  • Davis and Weinstein (2002). WWII bombing of Japanese cities (including Hiroshima and Nagasaki). Single equilibrium from fundamentals ⇒ population returns to trend; Gibrat random growth ⇒ permanent loss; multiple equilibria ⇒ fall to a low equilibrium. Cities returned to their prewar trend within about 20 years: fundamentals, single equilibrium.
  • TVA (Kline and Moretti 2014). 1933 agency, $30B (2000 dollars): dams, roads, a 650-mile canal, schools, flood control, cheap electricity to pull in manufacturing. Federal investment fell off after 1960. 1940–60: big gains in agricultural and manufacturing employment. 1960–2000: agriculture gains reversed, manufacturing gains intensified. Structural estimates: real agglomeration economies in manufacturing but not strong enough for multiple equilibria; slow adjustment rather than a new equilibrium; program roughly paid for itself; TVA manufacturing was headed for decline over the next 50 years. Does not rule out big pushes elsewhere.
  • Empowerment Zones (Busso, Gregory and Kline 2013). Round I (1994): Atlanta, Baltimore, Chicago, Detroit, New York, Philadelphia; about 700k residents in 60 sq. miles. Benefits: employment credit up to 20% of the first $15k of wages for employees who live and work in the zone; $100M SSBG per zone; $1–3B total treatment over six years. Assignment: tracts nominated locally, then HUD scored. Design: DiD using rejected and future-round zone tracts as controls, reweighted to match. Strengths: controls similar on observables and unobservables; cross-city comparison robust to spillovers. Weakness: exposed to city-wide shocks. Results: jobs up (zone jobs held by residents +15%), wages of zone residents working in zone +13%, commuters' wages ≈ 0, rents ≈ 0, housing values +28%, population ≈ unchanged. Many will commute in, few will move in, so local workers capture rents. Winners: resident workers, owners and landlords, maybe commuters. Losers: renters who cannot or do not work. Open: coordination/big push via seed money, dynamics, exit when designation expires.
  • US local economic development spending: $20–30B per year (Bartik 2002), little known about effects.
Traps
  • Pollution tax: both curves shift. Students who move only demand get the wage right by luck and the ambiguity wrong.
  • Rosen-Roback incidence is on landowners because workers are mobile with no surplus. A wage subsidy helps workers only to the extent supply is less than perfectly elastic.
  • EZ wage gains: residents working in the zone, not commuters. Rents flat, values up.
  • Davis-Weinstein supports a single equilibrium; TVA shows agglomeration without multiple equilibria. Neither is a clean win for big push, but neither rules it out.

L6 · Bid rent from the leftover principle

  • Market value is paid to own; land rent is paid per period to use. Rents are higher in dense areas and decline roughly exponentially; buildings are taller where land is dear; CBDs sit at the center; commuting is inward.
  • Leftover principle: willingness to pay for a site = total revenue − non-land costs. Bid rent per hectare = WTP / lot size. Farmland: depends on fertility. Urban land: depends on accessibility (freight cost to the highway, office workers' travel cost to the center). Competition drives profit to zero after paying rent, so variation in transport cost becomes variation in rent.
  • Office bid rent is steep: office firms exchange information face to face; by the principle of median location, total travel distance rises at an increasing rate with distance from the center.
  • Factor substitution makes bid rent more convex. Capital and land are substitutes (build up). Moving from 5 blocks to 1 block out: travel saving Δbid = $656, plus substitution saving $744, so Δbid exceeds the travel-cost change. Moving outward, substitution cushions the fall. Hence convexity.

L6 · Housing prices with and without consumer substitution

No substitution: every household uses \(h=1{,}000\) sq ft, has $800/month for housing plus commuting, commute cost \(t=\$50\) per mile. Locational indifference \(\Delta P\cdot h+\Delta x\cdot t=0\) gives a linear housing-price curve with slope \[\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta x}=-\frac{t}{h}=-\frac{50}{1000}=-\$0.05\text{ per sq ft per mile}.\] With consumer substitution, \(h(x)\) rises with distance because housing is cheaper there, so \(|\Delta P/\Delta x|=t/h(x)\) is larger near the center: the curve is convex. Housing producers' bid rent for land = \(P(x)Q-K\) (leftover principle), convex because \(P(x)\) is; factor substitution in housing (taller buildings on small lots) makes it more convex still. Density falls with distance for two reasons: cheaper housing ⇒ more sq ft per household; cheaper land ⇒ more land per sq ft of housing.

HW2 Q3 version. \(U=Y-pd-r(d)L+\alpha\log L\), reservation utility 1 at the edge. With \(L=1\): \(r(d)=Y-1-pd\), linear. With free choice of \(L\): FOC \(\alpha/L=r\) ⇒ \(L^*=\alpha/r\); indifference gives \(r(d)=\alpha\,e^{(Y-1-\alpha)/\alpha}\,e^{-pd/\alpha}\), exponential and convex, because households substitute away from land where it is expensive. Envelope theorem: \(\partial V/\partial r=-L^*\), \(\partial V/\partial d=-p\); differentiating \(V(r(d),d)=1\) gives \[r'(d)=-\frac{p}{L^*(d)}.\] Rents must fall with distance to compensate commuting; the curve is steeper where lots are small (center) and flatter where lots are large (edge).

  • Relaxing assumptions. Time cost of commuting is valued at 1/3 to 1/2 of the wage. Two earners at a common workplace double commuting cost and steepen the curve; different workplaces make the slope ambiguous. Non-commuting travel with concentrated destinations also makes it ambiguous. Better public services, lower taxes and amenities (clean air) raise housing and land prices, ceteris paribus.
  • Transport features of the classic city: manufacturers export on the intercity highway through the center or the beltway; office firms exchange information centrally; workers drive to work.

L7 · Facts on centralization and sprawl

  • Employment grows faster outside city centers; the monocentric model is "not so bad" but subcenters exist. Subcenter: density ≥ 25 workers/hectare and ≥ 10,000 jobs. LA: 28 subcenters, CBD density 90 vs subcenter average 45, subcenters hold 23% of metro jobs. Chicago: 20. Most jobs are dispersed, outside both the CBD and subcenters; many subcenters are specialized (localization economies); the CBD remains the place for face time.
  • Population: 36% of metro residents in central cities. 20% within 3 miles, 65% within 10; median residence 8 miles out. Paris center is 6× the density at 20 km, NYC 4×. Density gradient (% change in density per mile): Boston 0.13; US metros 0.05–0.15. Mills: gradient 1.22 in 1880, 0.31 in 1963. Three-mile share: 88% (1880), 24% (1963), 20% (2000).
  • Rise of the monocentric city: 19th-century scale economies and localization; intracity transport innovations (omnibus 1827, cable car 1873, electric trolley 1886, subway 1895) raised the feasible radius, hub-and-spoke concentrated jobs centrally; construction technology (balloon frame 1832, cast iron 1848 at 5 stories, steel 1885 at 11, elevator 1854 raising upper-floor bid rents); primitive freight (ship or rail intercity, horse wagons intracity, so output had to be near the port or rail terminal).
  • Demise: intracity truck (1910) twice as fast and half the cost of horse wagons, cutting output transport cost relative to moving workers, so firms moved toward low-wage suburbs; intercity truck (1930) and highways reoriented firms from ports to highways and beltways; automobiles; single-story plants; air freight; IT decoupled back-office processing (suburbs) from decision-making (CBD). Population decentralized with income growth (ambiguous: more housing demand pulls out, higher time cost of commuting pulls in), lower commuting cost, new suburban housing, and central-city problems.
  • Baum-Snow (2007). Did highways cause suburbanization, or the reverse? Instrument: rays of the 1947 planned interstate system, designed for trade and defense. Each planned ray predicts about half an extra actual ray by 1990; IV: an extra ray cuts central city population 12% by 1990; highways explain about a third of the decline. Constant-geography central city population −17% while MSA +72% (1950–90).
  • Sprawl facts: 1950–90 urban land grew 2.7× as fast as urban population; density: NYC 40/ha, LA 21, Phoenix 18, Chicago 15, Boston 14, surprisingly high in the West because land is expensive; Barcelona 28× as dense as Atlanta. Causes: lower commuting cost and higher income; culture; policy (underpriced congestion, mortgage subsidy, underpriced fringe infrastructure, minimum lot zoning). Glaeser and Kahn (2001): cars and trucks removed the orientation to central infrastructure; sprawl is ubiquitous regardless of income; housing and highway subsidies too small to matter. Europe: gas and auto taxes, protected small shops, farm subsidies, transit. Consequences: more land, same residential energy, 30% more travel, more greenhouse gas; farmland loss did not raise food prices. Transit needs about 31 people/ha; 60% of Barcelona within 600 m of a station vs 4% of Atlanta within 800 m.
  • Skyscraper game (Helsley and Strange 2008). Efficient height has MB = MC; "bragging rights" for the tallest ($200) induce a race: the leader builds 80 floors instead of 51 so the rival cannot profitably overtake; big gap between tallest and second (next tallest 11% shorter on average in the US); wasteful dissipation (total profit $1,800 vs $2,000).

L7 · Who lives where

  • Office sector at the center: office output (executives) is costly to move, manufacturing output in horse wagons was cheap. Segregated land use: employment area vs residential ring when moving output is costly relative to moving workers (streetcars).
  • Poor at the center. Central city poverty 20% vs 7.5% suburbs (2000); 19.1% vs 11.1% (2013). Traditional explanation: moving out saves on housing at a decreasing rate (convex price curve) and costs commuting at a constant rate; if the rich consume disproportionately more housing (4× housing with 2× income), their marginal benefit of distance exceeds their marginal cost more, so they live farther out. If both scale with income equally (MC and MB both double), the optimal distance is the same. Problem: the income elasticity of housing demand is about 1 or below, like commuting, so income alone cannot explain it.
  • Other explanations: new suburban housing, flight from central-city problems, exclusionary zoning, and public transportation (Glaeser, Kahn and Rappaport 2008): in old cities (NY, Philadelphia, Chicago) income is U-shaped in distance with the rich also at the center; in new cities (Atlanta, Phoenix, LA) income rises with distance; the poor live closer to the center where there is a subway (transit lets them avoid cars). Paris vs Detroit: central cultural amenities pull the rich in.

L7 · General equilibrium: streetcar (practice SA 2)

  • Assumptions: small open city (national utility fixed, population adjusts), no substitution, rectangular city. Prices: wage and rent.
  • Sequence: streetcar lowers unit commute cost ⇒ residential bid-rent tilts outward ⇒ residential territory and labor supply expand ⇒ excess supply lowers wage ⇒ lower wage shifts residential bid-rent down (slight shrink, supply shifts back a little) and business bid-rent up (firms have more purchasing power for central land) ⇒ CBD expands (O'Sullivan: 50%), labor demand rises ⇒ equilibrium: larger workforce, lower wage ($7/hr in the example), larger city. With variable density, rising rents raise density where rent rises, reinforcing the territory changes.

Traps
  • Slope of the housing-price curve is −t/h, where t is commuting cost per mile and h is housing per household. Convexity comes from substitution (h rises with x), not from anything about commuting.
  • Baum-Snow's instrument is the 1947 plan, not actual highways. The point of the IV is that planned rays were not drawn in response to local suburbanization.
  • The streetcar lowers the wage. Students often say "more workers, higher wages." Supply shifts out; wages fall.
  • Rich-in-suburbs needs the rich to consume disproportionately more housing; with elasticity 1 the model is silent.

L8 · Hedonics

  • Housing is a bundle; each characteristic has an implicit price. Hedonic regression \(y_i=x_i'b+e_i\): OLS needs \(E[x_ie_i]=0\), i.e., unmeasured characteristics uncorrelated with measured ones. Problem: more characteristics than observations; omitted variables (granite countertops attributed to bathrooms). No easy fix ("destroy a bedroom and see").
  • Typical signs: proximity to jobs, schools, transit, churches positive; crime, toxic waste, highway noise negative.
  • Davis (2004). Pediatric leukemia cluster emerged in Churchill County, NV, after 1999 (cumulative cases and newspaper articles jump). Compare house prices to Lyon County and Nevada: Churchill prices fell roughly 15 percent relative. The price decline over the change in risk gives a hedonic value of statistical life of $3M–$9.2M per child, near prior estimates. Price impacts reflect marginal willingness to pay.

L8 · Durability, quality ladder, filtering

  • Quality ladder. Normal deterioration moves a unit down; moderate spending holds it; large spending (renovation) raises it. Owner picks quality to maximize profit; older units are harder to maintain so quality drifts down; a higher price of quality makes higher quality optimal; a price decrease makes quality fall and eventually the unit is retired. Retirement: board up (short price dip), convert (profit from alternative exceeds conversion cost), abandon (otherwise). Abandonment when income < property tax; a flexible property tax prolongs use; abandoned buildings are eyesores and crime havens.
  • Supply elasticity. 97–98% of the stock is used; new construction is 2–3% per year; deterioration is slow; upgrading is costly, so supply is inelastic for long periods. Price increases raise quantity via new units, maintenance and renovation.
  • Moving costs create consumer disequilibrium: households tolerate mismatch (after an income windfall or as the unit decays) until it is large enough to justify a move.
  • Filtering. Quality falls about one step a year; occupants' income falls over time as the unit filters down the stepladder. High-end features do not always filter (MC Hammer's estate). Rising income raises demand for quality and the advantage of moving up into vacated units over costly upgrades.
  • Building ban (growth control) in a filtering market. Step 1: supply of high-quality units falls, their price rises, quantity falls. Step 2: fewer old high-quality units trickle down, so the supply of medium-quality units falls and their price rises. Step 3: the rich who were priced out of new units substitute to medium units, demand rises, price rises further and occupied medium units rise. Everyone pays more; some buy lower quality than they would have; the cost of the ban is borne throughout the market, not just by the rich.

L9 · Homelessness

  • HUD definitions: homeless = lacks a fixed nighttime residence; primary residence is a shelter, an institution providing temporary residence, or a place not meant for sleeping. Chronically homeless = disability plus continuously homeless one year, or four episodes in three years totaling 12 months.
  • Point-in-Time count: unduplicated one-night estimate of sheltered and unsheltered people, by Continuums of Care, in the last week of January. Trend: total ≈ 565k (2015) falling to a low near 2016–17, ≈ 580k (2020), 771k (2024). Sheltered fell 2015–2021 (to ≈ 326k in 2021, pandemic capacity cuts) and has risen since (≈ 497k in 2024). Unsheltered rose nearly every year (≈ 174k in 2015 to ≈ 274k in 2024); 2021 did not count the unsheltered. Chronic homelessness also rose sharply after 2016. Families with children fell through 2022 and rose in 2023–24. Homeless youth are mostly sheltered. Big variation by state and by race/ethnicity.
  • Support types: Permanent Supportive Housing (long-term, for people with a disability), Transitional Housing (up to 24 months), Rapid Re-Housing (temporary help into permanent housing). Shelter and PSH bed inventory has grown steadily.
  • Honig and Filer (1993): three measures (homeless per 100k, crowding, doubling up) across cities; strong responsiveness to rents (e.g., rent at the 10th percentile) and job creation; vacancy rates matter. Quigley et al (2001): housing market conditions (vacancy, rent) explain homelessness rates; changes in state mental patients and prisoners have surprisingly small effects (de-institutionalization is not the main story). von Wachter et al (2019): LA linked administrative data; service use spikes in the six months before first homelessness, so homelessness can be predicted and prevention targeted.

L9 · High-income housing policy: the mortgage interest deduction

  • $66B per year in lost revenue (vs $30B for all low-income housing policy). Interest is deducted from taxable income, so the subsidy rate equals the marginal tax rate (15%, 28%, 31%…). Benefit rises with income because the rate rises and mortgages are bigger; about 2/3 of the benefit goes to households above $100k. Budget-line picture: the deduction rotates the budget line out along the housing axis, more for high-bracket households. A tax cut has an ambiguous effect on housing consumption (more income, but smaller distortion).
  • Rent vs own. Identical $100k houses, zero maintenance, 8% interest: rent must be $8,000 (arbitrage). An owner pays $8,000 interest instead of rent, deducts it, saves $2,240 at a 28% rate. Bias toward ownership. Fix: tax imputed rental income (IRI): if D = IRI, tax is the same whether you own or rent. Simpler: eliminate the deduction. Rationales offered: neighborhood effects, stability, maintenance, civic participation (Glaeser and Shapiro 2003).

L9 · Rent control

  • WWII controls dropped everywhere but NYC; 1970s wave of regulations, some indexed to inflation and cost.
  • Glaeser and Luttmer (2003). Classical analysis: price ceiling below equilibrium cuts quantity to the supply at the controlled price; lost consumer and producer surplus (the triangle) plus a transfer from producers to consumers. But if the scarce units are allocated randomly rather than to the highest-value users, there is an additional loss from misallocation: the average consumer valuation of a unit is below the marginal valuation. NYC evidence: about 21% of apartments are in the "wrong" hands compared with the matching of apartment sizes to demographics in uncontrolled cities; the misallocation loss is as large as or larger than the classical deadweight loss.
  • Winners: occupants of controlled units, minus search costs, key money, and lower quality from weak maintenance incentives. Some residents displaced. Owners lose value. Externalities: landlords underinvest, and run-down controlled buildings reduce neighbors' incentives to invest.
  • Autor, Palmer and Pathak (2014). 1994 Massachusetts referendum ended rent control in Cambridge in 1995. Decontrolled units' transaction prices rose substantially; never-controlled units nearby also rose (spillovers); investment in decontrolled units rose, but part of the price effect reflects neighbors' behavior.
  • Diamond, McQuade and Qian (2019). 1994 SF law extended rent control to small multifamily buildings built before 1980. Compare tenants in pre-1980 vs post-1980 buildings, followed longitudinally. Rent control raised the probability of staying at the same address and in SF by several percentage points (larger for minorities, possibly via fewer evictions), but landlords cut rental supply by about 15% through condo conversion and redevelopment, raising citywide rents; the benefit to incumbent tenants is roughly offset by losses to future renters. Tradeoff: stability now, less affordability later.
Traps
  • Homelessness T/F: sheltered count fell through 2021 and has risen since; total count is at a record in 2024.
  • HMID subsidy rate = marginal tax rate, so it is regressive. "Same subsidy for all" is false.
  • Vouchers vs public housing: bang per buck 1 vs 0.24, but only if landlords accept and if prices do not move (Susin vs Eriksen-Ross).
  • Building ban raises prices in every submarket through filtering, not just at the top.
  • Rent control's extra loss (Glaeser-Luttmer) is misallocation, on top of the textbook triangle.

L10 · Property rights and transactions

  • De Soto (2000): undocumented property cannot become capital or collateral. Field (2007): Peruvian titling of 1.2M urban households raised household work hours 14%, cut children's hours in single-parent households 7.5%, raised the probability of working outside the home 28%; general-equilibrium growth evidence is thin.
  • English tradition: rights to own, exclusive possession, transfer, quiet enjoyment; limits: taxation, police power (zoning, codes), eminent domain. Estates in severalty (freehold indefinite, leasehold finite); concurrent (joint tenancy with survivorship; partnerships; corporations; REITs exempt from income tax). Typical: fee simple absolute, evidenced by a deed on file at the county (general warranty > special warranty > bargain and sale).
  • Selling: listing contract with a broker (exclusive right of sale via MLS; the listing broker works for the seller and is paid in proportion to price; open listing; exclusive agency); contract for sale (price, closing, financing, commission traditionally 6%, deposit, remedies); loan qualification; appraisal; closing. After the 2024 settlement: commissions cannot be advertised on MLS and the seller's agent cannot set the buyer's agent's pay; per-side commission stayed about 2.4% before and after, with small dips in late 2024 partly reversed in 2025.
  • Appraisal: market comparison (required: three comps, adjust for features the comp has and the subject lacks and vice versa), cost less depreciation, income capitalization (gross rent multiplier). Crude next to web estimates.
  • Levitt and Syverson (2008): agents' incentive is to sell fast. Agent-owned homes sell for 3.7% more and stay on the market 9.5 days longer, controlling for characteristics. Information asymmetry.
  • Hsieh and Moretti (2003): fixed commissions ⇒ inefficient entry; business-stealing externality. When land prices rise, the number of agents rises, houses sold per agent falls, agents' expected earnings are unchanged. The commission is dissipated in wasteful entry.
  • Mortgage math. Fully amortizing payment = MC × loan, \(MC=\dfrac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}\) with monthly \(r\) and \(n\) months; remaining balance = PV of remaining payments at the loan rate. Derivation: PV of an annuity \(b\) is \(b[1-(1+r)^{-n}]/r\). 2022: 30-year rate 3% → 6.5% took the annual MC from ≈ 0.051 to ≈ 0.077, monthly payments up about 50% for the same loan.
  • Crises. S&L crisis (1980s): interest-rate risk, fixed-rate assets funded by short deposits; a third of S&Ls failed; ARMs allowed; FSLIC folded into FDIC. Subprime crisis: bubble, oversupply in CA/NV/AZ/FL, prices dip in 2006, moral hazard from securitization (ownership and servicing separated), regulatory failure; Fed bought agency MBS 2008–10; millions underwater; four million foreclosures 2007–early 2012; robo-signing. Foreclosures spill over through comps and vacancy.
  • Campbell, Giglio and Pathak (2011): forced sales in Massachusetts. Foreclosed homes sell at a steep discount (about 27%), worse than sales after an owner's death; foreclosures lower prices of houses within 0.1 mile by a small but significant amount (about 1% per nearby foreclosure), negligible beyond 0.1 mile.

L11 · The Rosen-Roback model

  • Setup. Two markets (housing/land and labor); consumers get utility from the numeraire, land and amenities \(A\); the export good uses land, capital (same price everywhere), labor and \(A\). Productive amenity lowers cost, unproductive raises it (or is neutral). Equilibrium: \(V(w,r;A)=\bar u\) (locational indifference) and \(C(w,r;A)=1\) (zero profit everywhere). Under money-metric utility, disposable income net of housing varies across areas only because of amenities, so disposable income is an inverse proxy for quality of life.
  • Comparative statics. Totally differentiate: \(V_w\,dw+V_r\,dr+V_A\,dA=0\) and \(C_w\,dw+C_r\,dr+C_A\,dA=0\). Then \[\frac{dw}{dA}=\frac{\frac{C_r}{V_r}V_A-C_A}{C_w-\frac{C_r}{V_r}V_w},\qquad \frac{dr}{dA}=-\frac{V_A}{V_r}-\frac{V_w}{V_r}\frac{dw}{dA}=-\frac{V_AC_w-V_wC_A}{V_r\big(C_w-\frac{C_r}{V_r}V_w\big)}.\] Signs: \(V_w>0,V_r<0,V_A>0,C_w>0,C_r>0\); \(C_A<0\) productive, \(C_A>0\) unproductive. The \(dw/dA\) denominator is positive. Unproductive (\(C_A>0\)): numerator negative ⇒ \(dw/dA<0\); \(dr/dA\) ambiguous. Productive (\(C_A<0\)): \(dw/dA\) ambiguous; \(dr/dA>0\) (numerator \(V_AC_w-V_wC_A>0\), denominator negative). Also: if \(dw/dA>0\) then \(dr/dA>0\).
  • Reading cities. High rents + high wages: high amenity and high productivity. Low/low: neither. High rents + low wages: household amenities firms do not value (ocean view: Denver, Miami, San Diego, Portland). Low rents + high wages: productive amenities that are disamenities to households (smog, railroads: Atlanta, Cleveland, Pittsburgh). Eberts and Beeson 1980 classification.
  • Quality of Life index. Estimate \(P=c_p+a_{p1}A_1+a_{p2}A_2+bZ\) and \(W=c_w+a_{w1}A_1+a_{w2}A_2+dX\). Marginal WTP for \(A_1\) is \(a_{p1}-a_{w1}\) (extra housing cost paid plus wage given up). \(QOL=(a_{p1}-a_{w1})A_1+(a_{p2}-a_{w2})A_2\). Sensitive to the amenity list and to wage controls. Quality of Business Environment (Gabriel and Rosenthal 2004): same regressions, wage enters with a plus (firms accept high wages only where productive): SF is QOL 4 / QOBE 2, Miami 1 / 34, San Jose 14 / 1.
  • Albouy (2016). 2000 Census, 276 metros. Wage differentials \(\hat w\) and housing-cost differentials \(\hat p\) are city fixed effects from regressions with worker and housing controls; weights calibrated from shares (housing share 0.36, labor share 0.75, marginal tax ≈ 1/3, land shares 2.5% traded / 23.3% housing). \(\hat Q=0.32\hat p-0.48\hat w\) (quality of life: income share given up, Honolulu 0.21); \(\hat A^X=0.11\hat p+0.76\hat w\) (trade productivity, mostly wages, SF 0.29); total \(\hat\Omega=0.39\hat p+0.01\hat w\), driven almost entirely by housing costs since high wages signal productivity (+) and low QOL (−). Top total value: SF, Santa Barbara, Honolulu, Monterey, San Diego.
  • Diamond and Moretti (2026). Card and bank transactions, 443 commuting zones. Prices vary more for the poor (San Jose 66% dearer than Cleveland for low income, 35% for high); housing explains 90–95% of spatial price variation. College graduates' real consumption is unrelated to local prices (incomes offset); high-school graduates −0.195, dropouts −0.373 (Cleveland→San Jose cuts real consumption 8.7% and 16.6%). Why do the less-skilled stay in expensive places? Amenities, mobility frictions, or children's upward mobility. Measures market consumption, not utility.

L12 · Glaeser and Gyourko (2005): durable housing

  • Positive shock: price rises a little and construction follows over several periods; impulse response has a small price blip and a lasting quantity rise. Negative shock: price falls sharply, quantity unchanged; with linear depreciation the stock shrinks slowly and price recovers toward cost over a long time.
  • Puzzle: in many cities the median house price is below the cost of building a 1,200 sq ft home (hedonic: price = −155,954 + 4.56 × median family income + 1,441 × January temperature). Answer: the housing was built when demand was high; now demand is low, construction stopped, prices fell, and decay continues.
  • Evidence: population losses have huge price effects (coefficient ≈ 1.6–1.8), gains have small effects (≈ 0.1–0.2). Warm areas grew faster 1970–90 but prices did not appreciate more: easy to build. Losses are persistent (coefficient ≈ 1), gains transitory (≈ 0.45), since a loss only happens when demand falls far enough below the stock for depreciation to bite. College graduates are most sensitive: they flee losers and flock to gainers.
  • Conclusion: positive shocks ⇒ big population response, small price response; negative shocks ⇒ small population response, big price response; long periods of no construction, low prices and poor upkeep.

L12 · Mian and Sufi (2009): subprime

  • Question: why did subprime lending explode 2002–05 and why did defaults follow? Hypotheses: income (borrowers' prospects improved), supply (risk diversification, GSE subsidies), expectations (expected price growth lowers default losses).
  • Evidence: at the ZIP level, high-subprime ZIPs had falling relative income yet rising credit (the income story fails; Adelino, Schoar and Severino 2015 caveat: applicants within those ZIPs had higher incomes). Timing of credit expansion lines up with defaults two years later. The correlation between income growth and credit growth turned negative. Denial rates fell more in subprime ZIPs. Prices rose more in subprime ZIPs, but credit and defaults rose there even in high-elasticity areas with no price bubble, so expectations cannot be the story. The early-1990s rate decline produced no such pattern.
  • Conclusion: a supply shift. Credit flowed into communities, people took too much, defaults spiked.
Traps
  • Roback: name the amenity type before you sign anything. Consumption amenity ⇒ wages down for sure. Productive amenity ⇒ rents up for sure. The other variable is ambiguous.
  • Low wages + high rents is a nice place for households, not a poor one.
  • Glaeser-Gyourko: housing stock is the state variable. Decline is not the mirror of growth.
  • Levitt-Syverson: agents' own homes sell for more and take longer; the agent-client gap is the point.

Full notes for reference (not for memorizing)

L13 · Zoning

  • Comprehensive zoning begins 1916. Transport technology may have caused it: the truck moved industry to the suburbs and the bus brought low-income, high-density households between streetcar spokes; zoning excluded both.
  • Zoning as environmental policy: separates residents from pollution but only moves pollution around; the economist's alternative is a pollution tax or performance standards (traffic, noise, parking, views).
  • Fiscal zoning. Communities court firms that generate fiscal surplus and exclude households that generate deficits (large households in small dwellings). Minimum lot size exploits the complementarity of housing and land: \[s=\frac{v^*}{5r},\] target lot size = target property value over five times the land price per acre (assumes land is 1/5 of value). Example: $200,000/(5 × $80,000) = 0.5 acres. Atherton: 1-acre minimum, land ≈ $8M/acre, so the rule would imply \(v^*=\$40\)M; there land is most of the value. 2022 NIMBY episode: 348-home state allocation, 9 townhouse sites proposed and dropped; 2025 plan relies on 208 backyard cottages, lot splits, teacher housing, and 10-units-per-acre on designated parcels.
  • Space externality: a larger lot raises neighbors' utility, so unregulated lots are too small; MLS enforces reciprocity.
  • Legal tests. Substantive due process: legitimate public purpose, reasonable means (Euclid v. Ambler: health, safety, morals, welfare; benefits only, no cost test; SF Chinese laundries). Equal protection: Euclid (outsiders' interests irrelevant), Ybarra v. Los Altos (income discrimination OK); state activism: Mount Laurel (NJ fair share), Livermore (CA weighs outsiders). Just compensation: physical occupation yes; harm-prevention rule no; diminution-of-value rule rarely applied.
  • Houston has no zoning: voluntary covenants; similar distribution of industry and retail, more strip development, wide range of apartment densities, larger supply of low-income high-density housing.

L13 · Growth boundaries and permits

  • Winners and losers from a boundary. Workers everywhere lose: the uncontrolled city grows and its utility falls; in the controlled city competition raises rents until utility equals the uncontrolled level. The loss is the inefficiency of unequal city sizes. Landowners inside the boundary win (land prices rise). Homeowners inside win too. Municipal (not metropolitan) boundaries push congestion and pollution to neighbors. Portland's metro boundary expands periodically and is paired with density policy.
  • Density: with a minimum lot size, the boundary raises prices and reduces private space; allowing density inside softens the price effect. Open space near the city has benefits; the cost is higher prices and density ("cows on I-280").
  • Evidence. Katz and Rosen (1987): Bay Area prices significantly higher where growth management plans exist (dummy ≈ 0.17 log points), even between suburbs of the same city. Gyourko, Hartley and Krimmel (2018): Wharton index from an ICMA survey (who approves, supermajorities); hard to build on the coasts; highly regulated areas have high prices and more burdensome processes. Glaeser, Gyourko and Saks (2005): Manhattan permits collapsed from the 1960s while real prices rose; permits, not construction costs, drive the price increase.
  • Permit caps. With price $250k and marginal cost $160k, a permit is worth $90k; auctioning allocates efficiently and captures the rent; beauty contests do not. Development and impact fees can close the fiscal gap (tax revenue minus service cost), e.g., a per-job fee for transport infrastructure.

L14 · Tiebout, neighborhood choice, income segregation

  • Bay Area facts: within-city 90/10 ratio of tract per-capita income SF 3.7, Oakland 4.0, Berkeley 3.4. BA+ share 2000 → 2020–24: Berkeley 64→75%, SF 45→60%, Oakland 31→49%. Oakland's median tract went 19% → 53% BA+ while its 10th percentile tract went only 6% → 15%.
  • Tiebout sorting. Three voters with different park demands, $60/acre shared by head tax; majority rule picks the median voter's size. With three municipalities, sorting by demand gives everyone their preferred park: homogeneous communities accommodate diverse tastes (segregation as efficiency). Property-tax finance adds sorting by house size even with identical tastes (the "Gordos" leave Loisville to pay less for the same park): 3 → 9 communities.
  • Neighborhood externalities: role models, classmates, job information; positive externalities rise with income and education. Who gets the desirable neighbors?
  • Bidding model. Two neighborhoods of 100 lots, two income groups of 100. Start at 50-50 and ask if a small move toward segregation is self-reinforcing. Plot each group's rent premium for A against the number of high-income households in A. If the high-income premium curve is steeper (they value rich neighbors more), integration is unstable and the outcome is full segregation with a $30 rent gap. If the low-income premium curve is steeper, integration is stable. If the curves cross, a mixed equilibrium (70 high-income in A, $24 premium) with prices adjusting for locational equilibrium: households in B pay $24 less for an inferior mix.
  • Lot size and integration. Land is a normal good, so the rich buy bigger lots. Premium per unit of land: low income $5 on lot size 1 = $5; high income $8 on lot size 2 = $4. A pair of low-income households outbids one high-income household: integration. Minimum lot size forces low-income households onto lot size 2, so they are outbid: MLS promotes segregation.
  • Schools: achievement = f(home, peers, teachers, class size); fiscal sorting (demand for spending rises with income) and peer sorting (WTP for peers rises with income). SF/Oakland/Berkeley proficiency gaps across schools are enormous (Oakland top fifth 65% ELA proficient, bottom fifth 6%). Crime: elasticity of house value to crime rate −0.067; WTP for safety rises with income ⇒ income segregation. Oakland victim cost per resident $3,460 citywide, beats range $120–$17,600, homicide is 63% of cost.
  • Dissimilarity index. \[D=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^N\Big|\frac{b_i}{B}-\frac{w_i}{W}\Big|,\] the share of either group that would have to move for every tract to match the metro mix. US metro Black-white: 0.73 (1980), 0.63 (2000), 0.55 (2020); Hispanic-white 0.45, Asian-white 0.40 (2020). SF-Oakland 2020: 0.59 / 0.48 / 0.45. Black-white D fell everywhere, fastest in Oakland and Berkeley, where the Black population also fell sharply (Oakland 35% → 21%; SF, Oakland, Berkeley, Richmond lost 80k Black residents, −32%, while Antioch's Black population nearly tripled), so falling indices may reflect exit rather than integration. ACS-based D is biased up by sampling noise.
  • Preferences: most Black respondents prefer integration, most whites prefer segregation; Vigdor (2009) ideal share of Black neighbors: median 13% for whites, 33% for Blacks. Other causes: income segregation (small contribution), MLS zoning, racial steering, concentrated public housing; portable vouchers reduce concentration.

L15 · Empirical research on segregation

  • Cutler, Glaeser and Vigdor (1999). Dissimilarity and isolation (normalized Black share experienced by the average Black resident); a "ghetto" is D > 0.6 and isolation > 0.3. Both measures peaked around 1970 and have fallen since; ghettos grew more common until 1970. Before 1970, Black inflows strongly predicted rising segregation. Sources: ports of entry (Black preferences), collective-action racism (covenants, exclusionary zoning, violence: white preferences enforced collectively), decentralized racism (whites outbid Blacks for white neighborhoods). Blacks paid disproportionately higher rents in segregated cities until 1990, when the pattern reversed; segregation correlates with white, not Black, preferences. Conclusion: institutionalized racism gave way to decentralized racism; gradual desegregation since 1970, with price effects now favoring Black households.
  • Card, Mas and Rothstein (2008). Tipping: white households discontinuously leave neighborhoods that pass a city-specific minority share; cities with less tolerant racial attitudes tip at lower shares. Little effect on housing values or rents.
  • Cutler and Glaeser (1997). Segregation could hurt (spatial mismatch, cut off from peers) or help (keeps poor near rich Black households, sustains Black businesses). Empirically bad: Black outcomes worse relative to whites in segregated metros; survives IV (instruments for segregation using topography and local government fragmentation); slight benefits for whites; mechanism unclear.
  • Kling, Liebman and Katz (2007). MTO five-year results: public housing residents randomly offered nothing, a Section 8 voucher, or an MTO voucher restricted to low-poverty tracts. Better neighborhoods for a reasonable period; no adult economic effects; gains for girls (mental health, education, health); possible harm to boys. Not a refutation of spatial mismatch: distance to jobs did not fall.
  • Chetty, Hendren and Katz (2016). MTO children linked to IRS data: movers under 13 gain (earnings, college, neighborhood quality as adults); movers 13+ lose a little (disruption). Exposure effect ≈ $566 of age-26 earnings per extra childhood year in a good neighborhood. Move early.
  • Chyn (2018). Chicago demolished several large projects in the 1990s for maintenance failures; displaced families got Section 8 vouchers. Displaced vs non-displaced comparable at baseline; large short-run neighborhood improvement; children earned more, girls especially; boys less likely to be arrested; less dropout, especially when young at baseline; effects present even for teenagers, perhaps because the units were especially distressed.
Traps
  • MLS formula divides by 5r, not r: it assumes land is a fifth of value.
  • Growth boundary: rents rise inside, fall outside, workers lose region-wide, inside landowners win.
  • Lot size promotes integration in the unregulated model; MLS reverses it.
  • CGV: segregation peaked in 1970. Card-Mas-Rothstein: tipping points vary with attitudes. MTO: age 13 is the dividing line for children.

Papers, in the review sheet's format

The GSI's review sheet lists these 30 papers under seven headings with three boxes each: Question, Context/Approach/Model, Findings. The T/F section is built from the Findings box. Cover the Findings, say it aloud, then check the box only when you can.

Agglomeration

Bleakley and Lin (2012), "Portage and Path Dependence"

L1 · persistence
Question
Do cities persist after the natural advantage that founded them disappears?
Approach
Fall-line portage sites in the US Southeast, where rivers forced transshipment. Compare density at historical portage sites to nearby locations today; the portage advantage was obsolete by the late 1800s.
Findings
Portage sites remain unusually dense and show no decline relative to their surroundings. Consistent with increasing returns (agglomeration) sustaining activity after the founding advantage vanished: path dependence, coordination on an existing site.

Greenstone, Hornbeck and Moretti (2010), "Million Dollar Plants"

L2 · spillovers
Question
Do large new plants raise the productivity of incumbent plants nearby (agglomeration spillovers)? Why do counties bid for them?
Approach
Plant locations are not random. Use Site Selection magazine's reported winner and runner-up county for each MDP; losers are the counterfactual. Event study and DiD with plant FE, industry × year FE, pair FE, and winner-specific mean shift and trend break at opening. Output modeled as Cobb-Douglas in labor, capital, materials.
Findings
Incumbent plants' TFP in winning counties rises about 12% relative to losers within five years; trend break at the opening; effects grow (knowledge spillovers take time) and are strongest for plants sharing labor pools or technology with the MDP. Wages rise too, so part of the gain is competed away.

Ellison and Glaeser (1997), "A Dartboard Approach"

L2 · measurement
Question
Is geographic concentration of industries more than what random "dart throws" of plants would produce?
Approach
\(G=\sum_i(s_i-x_i)^2\) compares industry shares to overall employment shares; \(\gamma\) adjusts \(G\) for plant-size lumpiness using the Herfindahl \(H\). 4-digit manufacturing industries across states.
Findings
Clustering is real and varies a lot: most industries are more concentrated than random, but the \(H\) adjustment is small except for a few. Extreme cases: wine, fur goods. The index became the standard measure.

City size

Duranton and Puga (2001), "Nursery Cities"

L3 · diverse vs specialized
Question
Why do diverse and specialized cities coexist, and how do firms use them over the life cycle?
Approach
Model: firms search for their ideal process by prototyping; a diverse city lets them imitate many sectors without moving, a specialized city is cheaper to produce in. Data: French firm relocations across 341 metros; specialization \(s_{ij}\), diversity \(1/H\).
Findings
Firms, especially R&D-intensive ones, overwhelmingly relocate from diverse areas to areas specialized in their own sector. Diverse cities are nurseries; specialized cities are factories.

Holmes and Lee (2010), "Cities as Six-by-Six-Mile Squares"

L3 · Zipf
Question
Does Zipf's law hold for population in fixed geographic areas, or only for metro areas with elastic boundaries?
Approach
Replace metro definitions with a grid of 6×6-mile squares and estimate the rank-size exponent \(b\).
Findings
For fixed squares \(b\) is well below 1; Zipf fails. Zipf fits metro populations (\(b\approx1\)) because metro boundaries expand with growth, so the sprawl of political boundaries is what generates Gibrat's law.

Urban growth

Moretti (2012), The New Geography of Jobs

L4 · Great Divergence
Question
Why are US metros diverging in education and income?
Approach
Descriptive: college share by metro 1980–2008 and earnings of college graduates by metro. Supply story (amenities bid up land) vs demand story (productive places need graduates).
Findings
Variation in college share across metros is large and growing; earnings rose most where college share rose. Since earnings rise, demand factors dominate (a supply story would have workers accepting wage cuts). Good news for educated metros, bad for the rest. Diamond (2015) adds supply plus agglomeration.

Suarez-Serrato and Zidar (2014; AER 2016)

L4 · taxes
Question
Who benefits from state corporate tax cuts, and do they attract activity?
Approach
Changes in state business tax rates and apportionment rules; event studies and long-difference regressions of 10-year changes in outcomes on the net-of-tax rate; spatial equilibrium model with firm mobility.
Findings
Cutting state business taxes raises population (cumulative effect grows over a decade), the number of establishments (slope ≈ 3.9), wages (≈ 1.8) and rents/property values (≈ 2.2). Incidence is shared by firm owners, workers and landowners.

Moretti (2004), human capital externalities

L4 · spillovers
Question
Does a higher share of college graduates in a city raise other workers' wages and firms' productivity?
Approach
City college share and individual wages (instrumented, e.g., with land-grant colleges and lagged age structure); plant-level productivity on city college share.
Findings
Areas with more college graduates pay higher wages to everyone, including the less educated, consistent with complementarity across skills plus spillovers. Firm productivity rises with college share, strongest among economically similar firms.

Greenstone and Gallagher (2008) vs Gamper-Rabindran and Timmins (2011), Superfund

Question
How much do people value cleanup of hazardous waste sites, as revealed by housing markets?
Approach

Place-based policies

Wang (2011), Chinese Special Economic Zones

L5
Question
Did SEZs raise foreign investment and productivity?
Approach
DiD across municipalities by wave of SEZ designation (1980s onward); outcomes FDI, output, TFP, wages.
Findings
FDI rises sharply after designation in every wave; local productivity and wages rise. Pre-existing market failures (no private property rights) made intervention effective; part of the gain may be agglomeration. Lu, Wang and Zhu (2015) find county-level effects for later zones.

Kline and Moretti (2014, QJE), the TVA

L5 · big push
Question
Did the largest US place-based program have lasting effects, and is that evidence of multiple equilibria?
Approach
TVA counties vs comparable counties, decadal changes 1940–2000 (Oaxaca-Blinder reweighting), split before and after federal funding fell off around 1960; structural model of agglomeration.
Findings
1940–60: big gains in agricultural and manufacturing employment. After funding stopped, agriculture gains reversed while manufacturing gains intensified. Structural estimates: real manufacturing agglomeration economies but not strong enough for multiple equilibria: slow adjustment, not a new equilibrium. Program roughly paid for itself; TVA manufacturing was headed for decline over the next 50 years.

Davis and Weinstein (2002), "Bones, Bombs, and Break Points"

L5 · equilibria
Question
Single equilibrium (fundamentals), random growth, or multiple equilibria? A huge temporary shock can tell them apart.
Approach
WWII Allied bombing of Japanese cities as a shock to population; track city populations against prewar trends (Hiroshima and Nagasaki included).
Findings
Cities returned to their prewar trends within about 20 years: no permanent loss (rejects Gibrat) and no collapse to a low equilibrium (rejects multiple equilibria). Locational fundamentals, single equilibrium. Expectations can also sustain a return.

Busso, Gregory and Kline (2013, AER), Empowerment Zones

L5
Question
Effects and welfare consequences of the federal EZ program (Round I, 1994: six cities, ~700k residents, $1–3B).
Approach
DiD: EZ tracts vs tracts in rejected and future-round applications, reweighted to look alike. Census 1980–2000, LBD 1987–2000. Benefits: 20% wage credit on first $15k for employees who live and work in the zone, $100M SSBG per zone.
Findings
Jobs rose (zone jobs held by residents +15%); wages of zone residents working in the zone +13%, commuters' wages ≈ 0; rents ≈ 0; housing values +28%; population ≈ unchanged. Many commute in, few move in, so local workers and owners capture rents. Winners: resident workers, owners, maybe commuters. Losers: non-working renters. Deadweight loss small relative to transfers.

Baum-Snow (2007), "Did Highways Cause Suburbanization?"

L7 · IV
Question
Did interstate highways cause central city population decline, or did suburbanization cause highways?
Approach
Instrument actual rays through central cities with the rays in the 1947 national highway plan (built for trade and defense). Constant-geography central city population, 1950–90.
Findings
Each planned ray predicts about half an extra actual ray; IV: one extra ray cuts central city population about 12% by 1990. Highways explain about a third of the decline in central city population relative to metros (constant-geography central cities −17% while MSAs +72%).

Glaeser, Kahn and Rappaport (2008), "Why Do the Poor Live in Cities?"

L7 · transit
Question
Why are the poor concentrated near city centers?
Approach
Income by distance from the CBD in old vs new cities; the role of public transportation; test the income-elasticity-of-housing story.
Findings
The standard story needs the rich to consume far more housing, but the income elasticity of housing demand is about 1, like commuting, so it cannot explain much. Old cities (NY, Philadelphia, Chicago) show a U-shape with the rich also central; new cities (Atlanta, Phoenix, LA) have income rising with distance. The poor live closer to the center where subways exist: access to public transit, which lets them avoid cars, is the main driver.

Housing and housing policy

Davis (2004), cancer cluster and house prices

L8 · hedonic VSL
Question
How do housing prices respond to a sudden health risk, and what value of a statistical life does that imply?
Approach
Pediatric leukemia cluster in Churchill County, NV, emerging in 1999–2000. DiD in house prices vs Lyon County and the state index; risk measured from cases.
Findings
Churchill prices fell about 15 percent relative to controls after the cluster was publicized. Price decline divided by the change in risk gives a VSL of $3M–$9.2M per child, close to labor-market estimates. Price impacts reflect marginal WTP.

Baum-Snow and Marion (2009), LIHTC and neighborhoods

L9 · RD
Question
Does the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit add housing or crowd out private construction, and what does it do to neighborhoods?
Approach
Regression discontinuity: tracts with more than 50% eligible households get a 30% larger credit (Qualified Census Tracts).
Findings
The larger credit yields about 6 more low-income units per tract off a base of 7. LIHTC displaces new construction within about 1 km (roughly 20% crowd-out), about 60% in nearby gentrifying neighborhoods and none in low-demand areas. Raises turnover and median income in declining areas. Diamond and McQuade (2019): LIHTC raises values in low-income areas, lowers them in high-income areas, lowers crime.

Susin (2003) vs Eriksen and Ross (2015), vouchers and rents

L9 · price effects
Question
Do housing vouchers raise rents for everyone else?
Approach
Susin: cross-metro variation in voucher intensity and rents by income tercile. Eriksen and Ross: the large 1997–2003 expansion in vouchers, panel of metros.
Findings
Susin: vouchers raised low-income rents 16% (implied supply elasticity ≤ 0.38), middle-income 3.2%, high-income zero; $8.2B more rent paid by low-income non-recipients vs $5.8B subsidy, net loss $2.4B to low-income households. Probably omitted-variable bias (vouchers allocated to high-rent areas). Eriksen and Ross: no effect of the big voucher expansion on rents on average, so Susin was confounded (small effects only in the least elastic markets).

Autor, Palmer and Pathak (2014, JPE), end of rent control in Cambridge

L9
Question
What happens to prices and investment when rent control ends, including for never-controlled units?
Approach
1994 Massachusetts referendum ended Cambridge rent control in 1995. Event studies comparing decontrolled, never-controlled-near-controlled, and other units, 1988–2005.
Findings

Honig and Filer (1993) ; Quigley, Raphael and Smolensky (2001), causes of homelessness

HW1 Q1 · Five-island core-periphery with logarithmic transport cost (30 pts)

SetupEach island has \(N/5\) people with unit demand. Production cost \(C(x)=f+ax\). Transporting \(x\) units from the core to the four periphery islands in total costs \(T(x)=b+c\ln(1+x)\).
Solutions (a)–(f)

(a) Centralized: one factory makes \(N\) and ships \(4N/5\): cost \(f+aN+b+c\ln(1+\tfrac45N)\). Decentralized: five factories, no shipping: \(5f+aN\). Centralize iff \[b+c\ln\!\big(1+\tfrac45N\big)<4f.\] Scale economies (the four saved fixed costs) must exceed total transport cost.

(b i) Solve for \(c\): \[c^*=\frac{4f-b}{\ln(1+\tfrac45N)}.\] Production centralizes once \(c<c^*\) (requires \(b<4f\), otherwise never).

(b ii) \(\tilde T(x)=c\ln(1+x)\), \(\tilde T'(x)=c/(1+x)\). \[\epsilon_{\tilde T,x}=\frac{c}{1+x}\cdot\frac{x}{c\ln(1+x)}=\frac{x}{(1+x)\ln(1+x)}<1\] for all \(x>0\) (since \(\ln(1+x)>x/(1+x)\)). Transport cost rises less than proportionally with quantity: shipping has its own economies of scale, so doubling the shipped quantity less than doubles cost. That strengthens the incentive to centralize relative to a linear cost \(cx\), and the incentive grows with \(N\).

(c) If \(f,b,c\) all fall by the same factor \(\lambda\), the condition becomes \(\lambda b+\lambda c\ln(\cdot)<4\lambda f\): identical. No change in the centralization decision; only the relative levels matter. (Contrast the lecture's case where only \(T\) falls.)

(d) With linear cost, \(aN\) appears on both sides and cancels: the decision is independent of \(a\). With \(C(x)=f+ax^2\): centralized production cost is \(aN^2\), decentralized \(5a(N/5)^2=aN^2/5\). Now centralizing adds \(\tfrac45aN^2\) of production cost (diseconomies of scale in the variable part), so the condition is \(b+c\ln(1+\tfrac45N)+\tfrac45aN^2<4f\), and \(a\) matters: higher \(a\) discourages centralization.

(e) With \(n\) islands of size \(N/n\) and one possible core: centralize iff \(b+c\ln\!\big(1+\tfrac{n-1}{n}N\big)<(n-1)f\). As \(n\) rises, the saved fixed costs \((n-1)f\) grow linearly while shipping cost grows only logarithmically (and is bounded above by \(b+c\ln(1+N)\)), so centralization becomes more attractive; for large enough \(n\) it always occurs. More, smaller markets make a single core more valuable.

(f) Krugman's model has only scale economies and transport costs. Missing: natural advantages (ports, rivers, climate), agglomeration externalities beyond the firm (shared inputs, labor pooling, matching, knowledge spillovers), congestion and land costs that limit size, consumer preferences for variety and amenities, mobile labor with heterogeneous tastes, and history or expectations that select among equilibria (Bleakley-Lin, Davis-Weinstein).

HW1 Q2 · Labor pooling: San Francisco vs rural Arkansas (40 pts)

SetupA: supply perfectly elastic at \(w=1\). B: \(L^s=\eta w^2\). Production \(f(L)=zL^{3/4}\), \(z=\bar z\pm\varepsilon\) each with probability 1/2, price \(\phi>1\). No migration between A and B.
Solutions (a)–(f)

(a) Max \(\phi zL^{3/4}-wL\): FOC \(\tfrac34\phi zL^{-1/4}=w\) ⇒ \[L^d(z,w)=\Big(\frac{3\phi z}{4w}\Big)^4.\]

(b) With \(w=1\): \(L=(3\phi z/4)^4\), \(L^{3/4}=(3\phi z/4)^3\). \[\pi_A(z)=\phi z\Big(\tfrac{3\phi z}{4}\Big)^3-\Big(\tfrac{3\phi z}{4}\Big)^4=\Big(\tfrac{3\phi z}{4}\Big)^3\cdot\tfrac{\phi z}{4}=\frac{27}{256}(\phi z)^4.\]

(c) Market clearing: \(2a\,(3\phi z/(4w))^4=\eta w^2\) ⇒ \(w^6=\tfrac{2a}{\eta}(3\phi z/4)^4\) ⇒ \[w_B(z)=\Big(\frac{2a}{\eta}\Big)^{1/6}\Big(\frac{3\phi z}{4}\Big)^{2/3}.\] Wages rise with productivity \(z\), with the number of firms \(a\), and fall with the size of local supply \(\eta\).

(d) For any \(w\), \(\pi=(3\phi z/(4w))^3\cdot\phi z/4=\tfrac{27}{256}(\phi z)^4/w^3\). Substituting \(w_B^3=(2a/\eta)^{1/2}(3\phi z/4)^2\): \[\pi_B(z)=\frac{3}{16}(\phi z)^2\Big(\frac{\eta}{2a}\Big)^{1/2}.\] Decreasing in \(a\): more firms bid up the local wage.

(e) \(E[\pi_A]=\tfrac{27}{256}\phi^4\cdot\tfrac12[(\bar z+\varepsilon)^4+(\bar z-\varepsilon)^4]=\tfrac{27}{256}\phi^4(\bar z^4+6\bar z^2\varepsilon^2+\varepsilon^4)\). \(E[\pi_B]=\tfrac{3}{16}\phi^2(\eta/2a)^{1/2}\cdot\tfrac12[(\bar z+\varepsilon)^2+(\bar z-\varepsilon)^2]=\tfrac{3}{16}\phi^2(\eta/2a)^{1/2}(\bar z^2+\varepsilon^2)\).

(f) Both are increasing in \(\varepsilon\) because profit is convex in \(z\) (the firm expands when \(z\) is high and contracts when low: good news is exploited, bad news is cushioned, the labor-pooling logic). But A's profit is quartic in \(z\) while B's is only quadratic: in B a high \(z\) raises all firms' demand and bids up \(w_B\) (\(w_B\propto z^{2/3}\)), which eats part of the gain, and a low \(z\) lowers the wage, which cushions the loss. The wage response in B damps the profit swings, so the value of volatility is smaller in B than in the deep SF labor pool. That is why volatile, specialized firms cluster in A.

HW1 Q3 · Ellison-Glaeser index, conceptual parts

SetupState employment shares \(x_i\) for all industries and \(s_i\) for your industry, 2012 and 2017, 48 contiguous states.
Solutions (c), (e), (f)

(c) \(G=\sum_i(s_i-x_i)^2\): how much more unevenly the industry is distributed across states than employment as a whole. Zero if the industry mirrors total employment; larger when it concentrates in states with small overall employment shares. It measures concentration in excess of what the geography of overall activity would produce.

(e) Compare \(G_{2017}\) with \(G_{2012}\). Higher ⇒ more concentrated.

(f) Even random plant locations produce positive \(G\) if there are few, large plants (darts). \(H=\sum_j z_j^2\) measures plant-size lumpiness; \(\gamma=\frac{G-(1-\sum x_i^2)H}{(1-\sum x_i^2)(1-H)}\) nets out the \(G\) that random location with those plant sizes would generate. With 2-digit industries spread over thousands of establishments, \(H\) is close to zero, so the adjustment would be negligible here; it matters for industries with a handful of large plants (vacuum cleaners).

HW2 Q1 · Three-city free migration equilibrium (40 pts)

Setup\(u_A=2-p_A\), \(u_B=2-2p_B\), \(u_C=\tfrac32-p_C\), \(p_A+p_B+p_C=1\).
Solutions (a)–(e)

(a) Equalize utilities: \(2-p_A=2-2p_B\) ⇒ \(p_A=2p_B\); \(2-2p_B=\tfrac32-p_C\) ⇒ \(p_C=2p_B-\tfrac12\). Adding: \(5p_B-\tfrac12=1\) ⇒ \(p_B=0.3\), \(p_A=0.6\), \(p_C=0.1\). Common utility \(1.4\).

(b) Stable. All three utility curves slope down in own population (congestion dominates everywhere). Move a few workers from A to B: \(u_A\) rises and \(u_B\) falls, so they return; the same for any pair. Deviations are self-correcting.

(c) No. Aggregate utility at the equilibrium is \(0.6(1.4)+0.3(1.4)+0.1(1.4)=1.4\). The planner (part d) achieves 1.4375.

(d) Planner maximizes \(W=\sum_i p_iu_i(p_i)\) subject to \(\sum p_i=1\). Substituting the constraint and taking FOCs gives equal marginal social utility across cities: \[u_i(p_i)+p_iu_i'(p_i)=u_i(p_i)\big(1+\eta_i\big)\ \text{equal for all }i,\qquad \eta_i=\frac{p_iu_i'}{u_i}.\] Migration equates \(u_i\); the planner equates \(u_i(1+\eta_i)\). An interior free-migration equilibrium is optimal iff the elasticities are equal at it. Here \(\eta_A=-0.6/1.4=-0.43\), \(\eta_B=-2(0.3)/1.4=-0.43\), \(\eta_C=-0.1/1.4=-0.07\): C's elasticity differs, so the equilibrium is not optimal (C is too small: adding a worker to C costs little congestion). Planner: \(2-2p_A=2-4p_B=\tfrac32-2p_C\) ⇒ \(p=(0.5,0.25,0.25)\), \(W=0.5(1.5)+0.25(1.5)+0.25(1.25)=1.4375\).

(e) Higher utility in A at every share creates a utility gap; workers flow from B and C into A until utilities re-equalize at a higher common level. A grows. B and C both shrink, each by enough to raise its own utility by the same \(\Delta u\): \(\Delta p_B=-\Delta u/2\), \(\Delta p_C=-\Delta u\), so C (flatter curve, utility less sensitive to population) loses twice as many people as B. Forces: migration arbitrage, congestion in A rising as it grows, congestion relief in B and C as they shrink.

HW2 Q2 · Taxing jobs in Equalityland (30 pts)

SetupEach job yields $28,000 revenue, zero profits (demand perfectly elastic at $28k). Supply \(S(w)=4w\).
Solutions (a)–(g)

(a) \(w=28{,}000\), \(L=4\times28{,}000=112{,}000\). Flat demand at 28k, supply through the origin with slope 1/4.

(b) Firms pay \(w+6{,}000=28{,}000\) ⇒ \(w=22{,}000\), \(L=88{,}000\). Jobs fall by 24,000; the wage falls by the full tax (perfectly elastic demand means workers bear it all). Graph: demand net of tax shifts down to 22k.

(c) Revenue \(=6{,}000\times88{,}000=\$528\)M. Workers still employed lose \(6{,}000\times88{,}000=\$528\)M. Workers displaced (24,000) lose their surplus: triangle \(\tfrac12\times24{,}000\times6{,}000=\$72\)M. Firms lose nothing (zero profit before and after). Total cost $600M vs revenue $528M: deadweight loss $72M.

(d) No tax: $24k minimum is below $28k, not binding; same as (a). With tax: the market wage would be $22k < $24k, so the floor binds; the firm's cost becomes \(24{,}000+6{,}000=30{,}000>28{,}000\), so no job covers its cost: employment falls to zero. Graph: horizontal floor at 24k above the net demand line at 22k.

(e) High types: 20,000 workers, reservation wage $35k, revenue $41k. Firms hire a high type if \(41{,}000\ge35{,}000+\text{tax}\). No tax: all 20,000 hired at $35k; firms earn $6k rents on each. With the $6k tax: \(41{,}000=35{,}000+6{,}000\), still (weakly) worth hiring, so all 20,000 remain employed at $35k; the tax is paid out of firms' rents on high types. Low types: exactly as in (a)–(b): wage 28k → 22k, jobs 112k → 88k. Incidence differs by type: low types bear the tax in wages (elastic demand, upward supply), high types bear none (inelastic supply at the reservation wage, firms absorb it).

(f) High types earn $35k > $24k, so the minimum wage never binds for them, with or without tax. Low types: without tax, non-binding; with tax, binding and low-type employment goes to zero (as in d).

(g) No difference. Firms pay $28k (zero profit), workers take home \(28{,}000-6{,}000=22{,}000\), and supply \(4\times22{,}000=88{,}000\). Economic incidence depends on elasticities, not on who writes the check.

HW2 Q3 · Bid rent with and without land choice (30 pts)

Setup\(U=Y-pd-r(d)L+\alpha\log L\); reservation utility 1 (living with mom at the edge).
Solutions (a)–(d)

(a) \(L=1\), \(\log1=0\): indifference \(Y-pd-r(d)=1\) ⇒ \[r(d)=Y-1-pd.\] Linear bid rent with slope \(-p\): each mile costs \(p\) in commuting, so rent must fall by \(p\).

(b) FOC in \(L\): \(-r+\alpha/L=0\) ⇒ \(L^*=\alpha/r\). Indirect utility \(Y-pd-\alpha+\alpha\log(\alpha/r)=1\) ⇒ \(\log(\alpha/r)=\frac{1+\alpha-Y+pd}{\alpha}\) ⇒ \[r(d)=\alpha\exp\!\Big(\frac{Y-1-\alpha}{\alpha}\Big)e^{-pd/\alpha}.\] Exponential, declining, convex.

(c) Linear → convex. With a fixed lot, commuting cost must be absorbed one-for-one by rent. With a choice of lot size, households near the center shrink their lots when land is dear, which raises their bid per hectare there; far out they spread out, so rent per hectare falls more slowly. Substitution flattens the curve at distance and steepens it at the center.

(d i) \(V(r,d)=\max_L\{Y-pd-rL+\alpha\log L\}\). Envelope: differentiate the objective at \(L^*\): \(\partial V/\partial r=-L^*\), \(\partial V/\partial d=-p\).

(d ii) \(V(r(d),d)=1\) for all occupied \(d\). Differentiate: \(V_r\,r'(d)+V_d=0\) ⇒ \(-L^*r'(d)-p=0\) ⇒ \[r'(d)=-\frac{p}{L^*(d)}.\]

(d iii) Rents fall with distance because households farther out pay more to commute and must be compensated with cheaper land to stay indifferent. The slope is the commuting cost per mile spread over the land consumed: where lots are small (center) each extra mile of commuting must be offset by a bigger drop in rent per hectare, so the curve is steep; where lots are large (edge) the same \(p\) is spread over more land, so the curve is flat. Since \(L^*\) rises with distance, the curve is convex.
